## Beneficiary Designation Mistakes to Avoid: Protect Your Retirement Accounts and Life Insurance in 2026

You can spend thousands on an estate plan — a revocable trust, a pour-over will, carefully drafted powers of attorney — and still have the single most important wealth transfer decision undermined by a form you filled out in eight minutes during your first week at a new job.

That form is your **beneficiary designation**, and it controls where more of your money goes than your will does.

Beneficiary designations on retirement accounts (IRAs, 401(k)s, 403(b)s, TSP accounts), life insurance policies, annuities, and payable-on-death bank accounts **override your will and trust**. It doesn't matter what your estate planning attorney drafted. It doesn't matter what you told your family. If the beneficiary designation form says your ex-spouse gets your $800,000 IRA, your ex-spouse gets your $800,000 IRA.

The Supreme Court confirmed this in *Hillman v. Maretta* (2013) and *Kennedy v. Plan Administrator for DuPont* (2009). The beneficiary designation wins. Every time.

This article breaks down the most common — and most costly — beneficiary designation mistakes, who they hurt, and exactly how to fix them.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Beneficiary designations override your will and trust** — they are the actual transfer mechanism for retirement accounts, life insurance, and annuities, regardless of what other estate documents say.
- **Failing to update designations after major life events** (marriage, divorce, birth, death) is the single most common mistake and has generated billions in unintended transfers and litigation.
- **Naming your estate as beneficiary** on a retirement account eliminates the stretch IRA option, accelerates taxes, and exposes the account to probate creditors — it is almost always wrong.
- **Missing contingent beneficiaries** means assets default to your estate if the primary beneficiary predeceases you, creating the same problems as naming your estate directly.
- **Per stirpes vs. per capita** designation determines whether a deceased beneficiary's share passes to their children or is split among surviving beneficiaries — getting this wrong can disinherit an entire branch of your family.

## Why Beneficiary Designations Matter More Than Your Will

Most people assume their will or trust controls everything. It doesn't. For many Americans, the majority of their financial assets pass **outside** of their will entirely, through beneficiary designations.

Consider a typical household balance sheet:

- **401(k) or IRA**: $500,000–$2,000,000+
- **Life insurance**: $500,000–$2,000,000 death benefit
- **Roth IRA**: $100,000–$500,000
- **Annuities or deferred compensation**: variable
- **Taxable brokerage and real property**: variable

In many cases, retirement accounts and life insurance alone represent 60–80% of total transferable wealth. All of it passes by beneficiary designation. Your will handles the remainder — the house, the car, the personal property. The big money moves on a form.

And unlike a will, which typically goes through probate review and can be contested, a beneficiary designation is a contract between you and the financial institution. Absent fraud or incapacity, courts enforce it as written.

## Mistake #1: Naming Your Ex-Spouse (or Forgetting to Remove Them)

This is the most litigated beneficiary designation mistake in the country. Here's how it happens: you designate your spouse as beneficiary on your 401(k) when you start a new job at age 30. You divorce at 42. Your divorce decree says all retirement assets go to you. You remarry at 45. You die at 62.

Your ex-spouse from your first marriage gets the 401(k). Not your current spouse. Not your children. Your ex.

Why? Because you never updated the beneficiary designation form, and **ERISA-governed plans (401(k)s, 403(b)s, pensions) are required by federal law to pay the named beneficiary**. State divorce decrees that purport to revoke an ex-spouse's beneficiary status are preempted by ERISA for employer-sponsored plans.

For IRAs and life insurance (which are governed by state law, not ERISA), some states have enacted "revocation upon divorce" statutes that automatically revoke an ex-spouse's designation. But not all states have them, enforcement is inconsistent, and relying on an automatic revocation you haven't verified is a gamble with six- or seven-figure stakes.

### How to Fix It

Update every beneficiary designation within 30 days of a divorce. Every retirement account, every life insurance policy, every annuity, every POD account. Don't assume your divorce attorney handled it — in most cases, they didn't, because updating beneficiary designations with financial institutions is the account holder's responsibility.

## Mistake #2: Naming Your Estate as Beneficiary

This mistake costs families tens of thousands of dollars in unnecessary taxes every year, and it is shockingly common.

When you name your **estate** as the beneficiary of a retirement account (IRA, 401(k), etc.), three bad things happen simultaneously:

1. **The account goes through probate.** It becomes subject to probate court fees, delays, and public disclosure. Retirement accounts with named individual beneficiaries skip probate entirely.

2. **Creditors can reach it.** In probate, the account becomes an estate asset subject to creditor claims. In most states, an inherited IRA that passes directly to a named beneficiary has some degree of creditor protection under state law.

3. **Accelerated tax on the entire balance.** Under the SECURE Act's 10-year rule, a non-eligible designated beneficiary (most adult children) must withdraw the entire inherited IRA within 10 years. But when the estate is the beneficiary, there is no "designated beneficiary" at all — the account must be distributed even faster: within five years if the account owner died before their required beginning date, or over the deceased owner's remaining single life expectancy if they died after. Either way, the tax hit is compressed and often substantially larger.

### How to Fix It

Name individuals (or a properly drafted trust, when needed for control) as beneficiaries on all retirement accounts. The only time naming your estate may be appropriate is when there is a specific legal reason — and even then, a trust designated as beneficiary typically achieves the same control with better tax outcomes.

## Mistake #3: Forgetting Contingent Beneficiaries

You name your spouse as primary beneficiary. Your spouse predeceases you by six months. You haven't listed a contingent beneficiary. You die.

The account defaults to your estate, triggering all the problems described in Mistake #2 — probate, creditor exposure, and accelerated taxation.

This scenario is far more common than people realize. It also occurs in simultaneous-death situations (car accidents, natural disasters) and when the primary beneficiary disclaims the inheritance for tax planning purposes but there is no contingent beneficiary to receive the disclaimed assets.

### How to Fix It

Always name at least one contingent (secondary) beneficiary on every account. For most people, this means: primary beneficiary is spouse, contingent beneficiaries are children (or a trust for minor children). Review the contingent designation with the same rigor as the primary.

## Mistake #4: Getting Per Stirpes vs. Per Capita Wrong

These two Latin phrases control what happens to a deceased beneficiary's share, and confusing them can disinherit an entire branch of your family.

**Per stirpes** means "by branch." If you name your three children as equal beneficiaries and one of them predeceases you, that child's share passes to their children (your grandchildren). Each branch of the family tree stays intact.

**Per capita** means "by head." If one of your three children predeceases you, their share is divided equally among the two surviving children. Your deceased child's kids get nothing.

Most people intend per stirpes. Many forms default to per capita. Some forms don't even offer the option unless you specifically request it.

### How to Fix It

When completing or updating beneficiary designations, explicitly specify "per stirpes" if you want a deceased beneficiary's share to flow to their descendants. If the form doesn't include this option, attach a written addendum or contact the plan administrator. Some custodians (Fidelity, Schwab, Vanguard) now include per stirpes as a standard option on their beneficiary designation forms.

## Mistake #5: Not Coordinating Designations with Your Trust

If you have a revocable living trust as the centerpiece of your estate plan, your trust only controls assets that are funded into it. Retirement accounts and life insurance policies typically are **not** retitled into the trust during your lifetime (and doing so with an IRA would trigger a taxable distribution). Instead, these assets pass by beneficiary designation.

The coordination mistake happens when someone names individual beneficiaries on their retirement accounts that conflict with the distribution scheme in their trust. For example, the trust says everything is split equally among three children with specific conditions (age restrictions, spendthrift protections), but the IRA beneficiary form names only two of the three children because the form was filled out before the third child was born.

### How to Fix It

Review your beneficiary designations at the same time you review or update your trust — at minimum every two to three years, and after every major life event (birth, death, marriage, divorce, adoption, significant change in wealth). Ensure the designations align with your overall estate plan's intent.

For situations where you need the trust's protective provisions to apply to retirement account distributions (minor children, spendthrift beneficiaries, special needs beneficiaries), consider naming the trust as beneficiary — but only if the trust is drafted as a **"see-through" or "look-through" trust** that meets IRS requirements to preserve the 10-year stretch for eligible designated beneficiaries.

## Mistake #6: Ignoring State Community Property and Spousal Consent Rules

In the nine community property states (Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, Wisconsin) and in Alaska (which allows opt-in community property), your spouse may have a legal claim to a portion of retirement assets earned during the marriage — regardless of the beneficiary designation.

For ERISA-governed plans (401(k), 403(b), pension), federal law requires **spousal consent** before you can name anyone other than your spouse as primary beneficiary. This means even if you want to name your children or a trust, your spouse must sign a notarized waiver.

For IRAs and non-ERISA plans, the rules vary by state. In community property states, naming a non-spouse beneficiary without spousal consent on community property IRA funds can lead to the surviving spouse challenging the designation — and winning.

### How to Fix It

If you live in a community property state or have an ERISA-governed plan and want to name a non-spouse beneficiary, ensure you have documented spousal consent. Work with an estate planning attorney who understands both federal ERISA requirements and your state's marital property rules.

## The Annual Beneficiary Audit: A 30-Minute Task Worth Millions

The fix for all of these mistakes is the same: a systematic annual review.

Every year — ideally when you do your annual financial plan review or tax planning — pull up the beneficiary designations for every account:

1. **All retirement accounts** — 401(k), 403(b), IRA, Roth IRA, SEP IRA, TSP, pension
2. **All life insurance policies** — term, whole life, universal life, group policies through your employer
3. **All annuities** — fixed, variable, indexed
4. **All POD/TOD accounts** — bank accounts, brokerage accounts with transfer-on-death designations
5. **HSAs** — Health Savings Accounts also have beneficiary designations

For each account, verify: Is the primary beneficiary correct? Is a contingent beneficiary named? Is the per stirpes/per capita designation correct? Does the designation align with your current estate plan?

Keep a master spreadsheet listing every account, the custodian, the current primary and contingent beneficiaries, and the date last reviewed. Store it with your estate planning documents. Share it with your estate planning attorney.

This 30-minute annual review has prevented more unintended disinheritances and unnecessary tax bills than any other single financial planning action.

## The Bottom Line

Beneficiary designations are the most powerful — and most overlooked — wealth transfer tool in your financial plan. They control more money than your will, operate outside the probate system, and override nearly every other legal document you own.

The mistakes outlined here are not edge cases. They happen to doctors, attorneys, business owners, and financial advisors themselves. They happen because beneficiary designation forms are boring, easy to forget, and buried in the back of an HR portal nobody logs into after orientation week.

Fix them now. The cost is zero. The time investment is minimal. The downside of getting them wrong is catastrophic — and entirely preventable.
