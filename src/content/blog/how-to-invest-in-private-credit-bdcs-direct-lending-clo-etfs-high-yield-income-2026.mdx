## How to Invest in Private Credit: The Fastest-Growing Asset Class Most Retail Investors Are Ignoring

Private credit has quietly become a $1.7 trillion asset class — and it's projected to surpass $2.3 trillion by 2028. Yet most individual investors have never allocated a single dollar to it. If you're earning 4–5% in a high-yield savings account and wondering whether there's a better risk-adjusted option for the income sleeve of your portfolio, private credit deserves serious consideration.

Here's the reality: institutional investors — pension funds, endowments, family offices — have been pouring into private credit for years, attracted by yields that consistently run 200–400 basis points above comparable public fixed income. The difference in 2026 is that retail investors finally have accessible, liquid vehicles to participate: business development companies (BDCs), CLO ETFs, interval funds, and even some direct lending platforms.

This guide breaks down exactly how each vehicle works, what yields to realistically expect, the risks you need to understand, and how to position private credit within a diversified portfolio.

## What Is Private Credit and Why Does It Matter Now?

Private credit refers to loans and debt instruments issued outside of public markets — meaning they aren't traded on stock or bond exchanges. Instead, specialized lenders originate loans directly to middle-market companies (typically businesses with $10M–$1B in revenue) that may not have access to, or prefer to avoid, the public debt markets.

### Why Private Credit Has Exploded

Three structural forces have driven the asset class's rapid growth:

**1. Bank retreat from middle-market lending.** Post-2008 banking regulations (Basel III, Dodd-Frank) made it more expensive for banks to hold risky loans on their balance sheets. Private credit managers filled the void, and this structural shift isn't reversing.

**2. Higher base rates favor floating-rate lenders.** Most private credit loans are floating-rate, benchmarked to SOFR. When the Fed raised rates from near-zero to 5%+ between 2022 and 2024, private credit yields surged accordingly — and even with modest cuts in 2025–2026, base rates remain elevated by historical standards.

**3. Borrower preference for speed and certainty.** Middle-market companies often prefer private credit because deals close faster, terms are more flexible, and there's no public disclosure requirement. For borrowers, the premium they pay over bank debt buys real operational advantages.

For investors, this translates to an asset class with structurally higher yields, floating-rate protection, and lower correlation to public markets — characteristics that are especially valuable in 2026's environment of persistent inflation and uncertain rate trajectories.

## Vehicle #1: Business Development Companies (BDCs)

BDCs are the most direct and liquid way for retail investors to access private credit. They're publicly traded companies (regulated under the Investment Company Act of 1940) that lend to and invest in middle-market businesses.

### How BDCs Work

A BDC raises capital from public shareholders and uses it — along with modest leverage (typically 1.0–1.25x debt-to-equity) — to originate or purchase private loans. By law, BDCs must distribute at least 90% of taxable income to shareholders, which is why they pay such high dividends.

### What Yields to Expect

In 2026's rate environment, the top-performing BDCs are generating **total returns of 9–13% annually**, with dividend yields concentrated in the 9–11% range. These are not promotional rates — they're a function of the underlying loan yields (SOFR + 500–650bps spreads on first-lien loans) minus operating expenses and credit losses.

### Top BDCs to Research

Here are the largest and most established publicly traded BDCs by market capitalization:

- **Ares Capital (ARCC)** — The largest publicly traded BDC with a $13B+ portfolio. Conservative underwriting, strong track record through multiple cycles, and a history of consistent dividends. Dividend yield approximately 9%.
- **Blue Owl Capital Corporation (OBDC)** — Focuses on upper-middle-market direct lending with a defensive portfolio. Merged with Blue Owl Technology Finance in 2024, creating scale advantages. Yield approximately 10%.
- **Golub Capital BDC (GBDC)** — Specializes in one-stop lending (combined first-lien and second-lien) to sponsor-backed companies. Lower volatility historically compared to peers. Yield approximately 10%.
- **FS KKR Capital (FSK)** — Managed by KKR's credit platform, providing access to institutional-quality deal flow. Higher yield but also higher historical volatility. Yield approximately 12%.
- **Main Street Capital (MAIN)** — Unique model that includes both debt and equity investments in lower-middle-market companies. Pays monthly dividends and has a strong track record of NAV growth. Yield approximately 8%.

### Key Risks with BDCs

**Credit risk is the primary concern.** BDCs lend to companies that are, by definition, below investment-grade. Default rates in the middle market have historically averaged 2–4% annually, but can spike during recessions. In a severe downturn, BDC NAVs can decline 20–30%.

**Leverage amplifies both returns and losses.** Most BDCs operate with regulatory leverage of 1.0–1.25x. This means a 5% loss in the loan portfolio translates to roughly a 10% hit to NAV.

**Fee drag matters.** BDCs typically charge a 1.5% base management fee plus a 17.5–20% incentive fee on income above a hurdle rate. Over time, these fees meaningfully reduce net returns compared to what the underlying portfolio generates.

**Interest rate sensitivity cuts both ways.** While floating-rate loans benefit from higher rates, a rapid rate decline would compress BDC yields — and if rate cuts coincide with economic weakness, you could face both lower income and higher defaults simultaneously.

## Vehicle #2: CLO ETFs

Collateralized Loan Obligation (CLO) ETFs have emerged as one of the most popular new fixed-income products in 2026, gathering over $20 billion in assets across the category.

### How CLOs Work

A CLO pools hundreds of leveraged loans (typically to large, sub-investment-grade companies) into a single vehicle, then issues tranches of debt securities — from AAA-rated (safest, lowest yield) down to equity (riskiest, highest yield). Investors in each tranche get paid in order of seniority.

### Why CLO ETFs Are Compelling

CLO ETFs offer several advantages over BDCs:

- **Diversification:** A single CLO may hold 200–300 underlying loans. A CLO ETF holds multiple CLOs, giving you exposure to thousands of loans.
- **Tranche selection:** You can choose your risk level — AAA CLO ETFs yield 5.5–6.5% with minimal credit risk, while BB-rated CLO ETFs yield 8–10%.
- **Daily liquidity:** Unlike traditional CLO investments (which are institutional and illiquid), ETFs trade on exchanges.
- **Lower fees:** CLO ETF expense ratios typically run 0.25–0.50%, dramatically less than BDC fees.

### Notable CLO ETFs

- **Janus Henderson AAA CLO ETF (JAAA)** — The largest CLO ETF, focused exclusively on AAA-rated tranches. Extremely low credit risk with a yield of approximately 5.8%. Think of it as a credit-enhanced money market alternative.
- **Janus Henderson B-BBB CLO ETF (JBBB)** — Mezzanine tranches offering higher yield (approximately 7.5%) with moderate credit risk.
- **VanEck CLO ETF (CLOI)** — Invests across investment-grade CLO tranches (AAA to BBB) with a yield of approximately 6.5%.
- **Panagram BBB-B CLO ETF (CLOZ)** — Focuses on lower-rated CLO tranches for higher yield (approximately 9%), accepting more credit risk.
- **BlackRock AAA CLO ETF (CLOA)** — BlackRock's entry into the space, similar profile to JAAA with competitive expense ratios.

### CLO ETF Risks

**Complexity risk is real.** CLOs are structured products, and many investors don't fully understand the waterfall mechanics, overcollateralization tests, or what happens when underlying loan defaults exceed certain thresholds.

**Liquidity mismatch in stress.** The underlying leveraged loans are less liquid than the ETF wrapper suggests. In a market panic, CLO ETF prices can temporarily deviate significantly from NAV.

**Spread compression.** AAA CLO spreads have tightened meaningfully in 2025–2026 as the asset class has attracted massive inflows. Current yields may not persist if spreads continue to narrow.

## Vehicle #3: Interval Funds and Non-Traded Funds

For investors willing to sacrifice some liquidity, interval funds and non-traded vehicles offer access to private credit strategies that more closely resemble what institutions buy.

### How Interval Funds Work

Interval funds are SEC-registered, but unlike ETFs or mutual funds, they only offer periodic redemptions — typically quarterly, and limited to 5–25% of fund shares. This structure allows managers to hold truly illiquid private loans without worrying about forced selling during market stress.

### Notable Interval Funds in Private Credit

- **Cliffwater Corporate Lending Fund (CCLFX)** — One of the largest private credit interval funds, investing directly in middle-market loans. Net yield approximately 8.5% with quarterly liquidity.
- **Apollo Diversified Credit Fund (CRDTX)** — Broad private credit exposure managed by Apollo's credit platform. Targets a net return of 8–10%.
- **PIMCO Flexible Credit Income Fund (PFLEX)** — Combines public and private credit for a blended yield approach, managed by one of the world's largest fixed-income managers.

### Interval Fund Considerations

**Liquidity constraints are binding.** You can't sell whenever you want — and during periods of high redemption requests, you may receive only a portion of your requested redemption.

**Valuation opacity.** Unlike publicly traded BDCs where the market prices shares daily, interval fund NAVs are calculated by the manager based on model-derived valuations. This can mask true volatility.

**Higher minimums.** Many interval funds require $25,000–$50,000 minimum investments, though some are available through platforms like Schwab or Fidelity with lower thresholds.

## Tax Implications: Where to Hold Private Credit

Tax treatment is a critical consideration that many income investors overlook.

### BDC Dividends

BDC dividends are generally taxed as **ordinary income** at your marginal federal rate (up to 37% plus the 3.8% NIIT). Some portion of BDC dividends may qualify as return of capital or long-term capital gains, but the majority is ordinary income. This makes BDCs particularly well-suited for tax-advantaged accounts (IRA, 401k, Roth IRA).

### CLO ETF Income

CLO ETF distributions are also generally ordinary income, as the underlying leveraged loans pay floating-rate interest. The same logic applies: hold them in tax-advantaged accounts when possible.

### A Tax-Smart Allocation Framework

| Account Type | Best Private Credit Vehicle | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Roth IRA | High-yield BDCs (FSK, OBDC) | Tax-free growth on highest-yielding vehicles |
| Traditional IRA/401k | BDCs, mezzanine CLO ETFs | Tax deferral on ordinary income |
| Taxable account | AAA CLO ETFs (JAAA, CLOA) | Lower yield = lower tax drag; potential for some return of capital |

## Portfolio Allocation: How Much Private Credit Is Enough?

The right allocation depends on your income needs, risk tolerance, and overall portfolio construction. Here's a framework based on investor profiles:

### Conservative Income Investor (5% Allocation)
- 3% in AAA CLO ETFs (JAAA or CLOA)
- 2% in a large, established BDC (ARCC or MAIN)
- Goal: Enhance fixed-income yield without significant credit risk

### Moderate Income Investor (10% Allocation)
- 4% in AAA/AA CLO ETFs
- 3% in diversified BDC exposure (ARCC + OBDC)
- 3% in a mezzanine CLO ETF (JBBB or CLOZ)
- Goal: Meaningful income enhancement with diversified credit exposure

### Aggressive Income Investor (15% Allocation)
- 5% in BDCs across multiple managers
- 5% in mezzanine/high-yield CLO ETFs
- 5% in an interval fund (CCLFX or similar)
- Goal: Maximize income from private credit while maintaining some liquidity

### Important Sizing Principles

**Don't chase yield into concentration.** A 15% allocation to private credit is plenty for most portfolios — beyond that, you're taking on meaningful idiosyncratic credit risk.

**Layer your liquidity.** Combine daily-liquid ETFs with less-liquid interval funds so you always have access to some portion of your private credit allocation.

**Complement, don't replace, traditional fixed income.** Private credit is not a substitute for Treasury bonds or investment-grade corporates in the "ballast" portion of your portfolio. It belongs alongside those positions, not instead of them.

## Red Flags to Watch in Private Credit

As the asset class has grown, so have the risks of excess. Here's what to monitor:

**Spread compression.** When too much capital chases the same deals, lenders accept weaker terms and lower spreads. This is a late-cycle dynamic that typically precedes rising defaults.

**Covenant erosion.** "Covenant-lite" loans — which lack the traditional protections that let lenders intervene before a borrower's situation deteriorates — now represent the majority of the market. This means losses, when they come, tend to be larger.

**Manager selection matters enormously.** The difference between a top-quartile and bottom-quartile private credit manager can be 400+ basis points annually. This isn't like buying an S&P 500 index fund where the product is commoditized — in private credit, who you invest with is one of the biggest determinants of your outcome.

**Watch for deteriorating credit metrics.** Rising payment-in-kind (PIK) income — where borrowers pay interest with more debt rather than cash — is a canary in the coal mine. If a BDC's PIK income is climbing as a percentage of total investment income, that's a sign its borrowers are struggling.

## The Bottom Line

Private credit in 2026 offers something increasingly rare: yields of 8–12% from an asset class with structural tailwinds and improving accessibility for individual investors. BDCs and CLO ETFs have made it possible to build meaningful private credit exposure in a standard brokerage or retirement account, without the lockups and minimums that previously limited this space to institutions.

But the key word is "meaningful" — not "maximum." Private credit should be a complement to your existing fixed-income allocation, sized appropriately for your risk tolerance and held in tax-efficient accounts. The investors who benefit most from this asset class are those who understand what they own, maintain disciplined position sizing, and resist the temptation to stretch for every last basis point of yield.

The structural forces driving private credit — bank disintermediation, floating-rate mechanics, borrower preference — are durable. This isn't a trade; it's an allocation that belongs in your long-term income strategy.
