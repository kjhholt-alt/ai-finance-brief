## Rolling Over a 401(k) to an IRA Is One of the Biggest Financial Moves You'll Make — Don't Blow It

Every year, millions of Americans leave a job and face the same question: what do I do with my old 401(k)? The answer, for most people, is to roll it over into an Individual Retirement Account. But the difference between doing it correctly and making a single procedural mistake can cost you thousands — sometimes tens of thousands — in unnecessary taxes and penalties.

In 2026, with 401(k) balances at record highs (Fidelity reports the average balance hit $134,100 in Q3 2026, with balances for workers aged 55-64 averaging $244,700), the stakes of getting this right have never been higher. A botched rollover on a quarter-million-dollar account isn't a rounding error — it's a financial catastrophe.

Here's exactly how to execute a 401(k) to IRA rollover cleanly, the traps that catch even sophisticated investors, and the strategies that can save you serious money.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **A direct rollover (trustee-to-trustee transfer) is almost always the right move** — your old plan sends the money straight to your new IRA custodian, with no tax withholding and no risk of missing the 60-day deadline.
- **An indirect rollover triggers mandatory 20% federal tax withholding** — you get a check for 80% of your balance, and you must come up with the other 20% from your own pocket within 60 days or it's treated as a taxable distribution.
- **You get one indirect rollover per 12-month period** across all your IRAs (the once-per-year rule). Violating this creates a taxable distribution and potential excess contribution penalties.
- **Roth 401(k) balances must roll into a Roth IRA** — mixing Roth and pre-tax money in the wrong account type triggers immediate taxation.
- **Company stock in your 401(k) may qualify for Net Unrealized Appreciation (NUA) treatment** — rolling it into an IRA could actually cost you money compared to taking a distribution.

## Why You'd Roll Over a 401(k) to an IRA

Before diving into the mechanics, let's be clear about why this move makes sense for most people.

### More Investment Options

The average 401(k) plan offers 15-30 investment options. An IRA at Fidelity, Schwab, or Vanguard gives you access to thousands of ETFs, individual stocks, bonds, REITs, and more. If your old 401(k) is loaded with high-fee target-date funds charging 0.50-0.80% annually, moving to a self-directed IRA where you can build a three-fund portfolio at 0.03-0.05% saves you real money over decades.

### Lower Fees

Many 401(k) plans charge administrative fees, recordkeeping fees, and revenue-sharing arrangements that can total 0.50-1.50% annually — costs that are often invisible to participants. IRAs at major brokerages typically have zero account fees and access to commission-free trading.

### Consolidation

If you've had three or four jobs, you might have 401(k) accounts scattered across multiple plan administrators. Consolidating into a single IRA simplifies your financial life, makes rebalancing easier, and reduces the chance of losing track of old accounts. (The Department of Labor estimates there are roughly 29 million forgotten 401(k) accounts holding over $1.65 trillion in assets.)

### Estate Planning Flexibility

IRAs offer more flexible beneficiary designation options than most 401(k) plans, including the ability to name contingent beneficiaries, set up per stirpes distributions, and in some cases use trust structures that aren't available in employer plans.

## Direct Rollover vs. Indirect Rollover: The Critical Distinction

This is where most mistakes happen. The IRS gives you two ways to move 401(k) money into an IRA, and they are not equivalent.

### Direct Rollover (Trustee-to-Trustee Transfer)

**How it works:** You tell your old 401(k) plan administrator to send the money directly to your new IRA custodian. The check is made payable to the IRA custodian "for the benefit of" (FBO) you — for example, "Fidelity Investments FBO Jane Smith IRA." You never have personal access to the funds.

**Tax consequences:** None. No withholding, no taxable event, no reporting headaches (beyond receiving a 1099-R coded as a rollover).

**Time limit:** None. The money goes straight from one institution to the other.

**This is the right choice for 95%+ of people.** It's cleaner, safer, and eliminates virtually all of the tax traps we'll discuss below.

### Indirect Rollover (60-Day Rollover)

**How it works:** Your old 401(k) plan writes a check payable to you personally. You deposit it into your bank account, and then you have exactly 60 calendar days to deposit the full amount (not just what you received) into an IRA.

**Tax consequences:** This is where it gets dangerous.

Your old plan is **required by law to withhold 20% for federal taxes** on the distribution. So if you're rolling over a $200,000 401(k), you'll receive a check for $160,000. To complete the rollover and avoid taxes, you need to deposit $200,000 into your IRA within 60 days — meaning you have to come up with $40,000 from your own pocket to replace the withheld amount.

If you only deposit the $160,000 you received, the missing $40,000 is treated as a taxable distribution. If you're under 59½, you'll also owe a 10% early withdrawal penalty on that $40,000. For someone in the 24% federal tax bracket, that's $9,600 in income tax plus a $4,000 penalty — $13,600 gone because of a procedural choice.

You'll eventually get the $40,000 back as a tax refund when you file your return (since the withholding was applied against taxes you don't actually owe, assuming you completed the rollover). But you need to front that cash in the meantime.

**Time limit:** Exactly 60 calendar days. Not business days. If day 60 falls on a weekend or holiday, the IRS does not give you an extension (though there are limited hardship waivers under Revenue Procedure 2016-47 and the more recent self-certification process under Revenue Procedure 2020-46).

## The Once-Per-Year Rule: A Trap for Multiple IRA Owners

The IRS limits you to **one indirect (60-day) rollover in any 12-month period** across all of your traditional IRAs. This rule, clarified by the Tax Court in Bobrow v. Commissioner (2014) and subsequently adopted by the IRS in Announcement 2014-15, applies in aggregate — not per account.

This means if you do an indirect rollover from IRA #1 to IRA #2 in March, you cannot do another indirect rollover involving any of your IRAs until the following March. Violating this rule means the second rollover is treated as an excess contribution (subject to a 6% penalty per year until corrected) and may also trigger a taxable distribution.

**The workaround:** Direct rollovers (trustee-to-trustee transfers) are unlimited. The once-per-year rule does not apply to them. This is yet another reason to always choose a direct rollover.

## Step-by-Step: How to Execute a Direct Rollover

Here's the exact process, in order.

### Step 1: Open Your New IRA

Before initiating the rollover, open an IRA at your chosen custodian (Fidelity, Schwab, Vanguard, etc.). This takes 10-15 minutes online. Make sure you open the right account type:

- **Traditional 401(k) → Traditional IRA** (tax-free rollover)
- **Roth 401(k) → Roth IRA** (tax-free rollover)
- **Traditional 401(k) → Roth IRA** (this is a Roth conversion — the entire amount becomes taxable income in the year of conversion)

### Step 2: Contact Your Old 401(k) Plan Administrator

Call the number on your 401(k) statement or log into the plan's website. Request a **direct rollover** to your new IRA. You'll need to provide:

- Your new IRA account number
- The receiving custodian's name and mailing address (or wire/ACH instructions)
- Whether you want to roll over the full balance or a partial amount

Many plan administrators now offer online rollover processing, but some still require paper forms with a medallion signature guarantee. Budget 1-3 weeks for processing.

### Step 3: Decide What to Do with Investments

Your 401(k) investments will typically be **liquidated to cash** before the rollover, since the receiving IRA won't hold the same proprietary funds. This means your money will be out of the market for a few days to a few weeks during the transfer — a risk in volatile markets.

Some rollovers can be done "in kind" if both the old plan and new IRA hold the same securities (common with company stock), but this is rare for mutual fund-based 401(k) plans.

### Step 4: Verify Receipt and Reinvest

Once the funds arrive in your IRA (typically 3-10 business days for electronic transfers, 2-4 weeks for checks), immediately reinvest the cash according to your target asset allocation. Cash sitting uninvested in an IRA is one of the most common — and most expensive — mistakes in retirement planning.

### Step 5: Confirm Tax Reporting

You'll receive a 1099-R from your old plan in January of the following year. Code G (direct rollover) or code H (Roth direct rollover) in Box 7 tells the IRS this was a tax-free rollover. Report this on your tax return, but verify the coding is correct — errors here are surprisingly common and can trigger an unnecessary IRS inquiry.

## Five Costly 401(k) Rollover Mistakes

### Mistake #1: Forgetting About the 20% Withholding on Indirect Rollovers

We covered this above, but it bears repeating because it's the single most common and most expensive mistake. If you take an indirect rollover and don't replace the 20% withholding out of pocket, you've created an accidental taxable distribution.

### Mistake #2: Missing the 60-Day Window

Life happens. You get the check, you put it on your desk, you forget. Sixty-one days later, you've got a fully taxable distribution. The IRS does offer a self-certification procedure for certain qualifying reasons (death, disability, hospitalization, incarceration, postal error, and a few others), but "I forgot" or "I was busy" doesn't qualify.

### Mistake #3: Rolling Over When You Should Use NUA

If your 401(k) holds **employer stock** with significant unrealized appreciation, rolling it into an IRA could be a tax mistake. Under the Net Unrealized Appreciation (NUA) rules, you can take a lump-sum distribution of the company stock, pay ordinary income tax only on the cost basis, and then pay long-term capital gains rates (currently 15-20%) on the appreciation when you eventually sell.

For example, if you hold $300,000 in company stock with a $50,000 cost basis, the NUA strategy means paying ordinary income tax on $50,000 and capital gains tax on $250,000 when sold — versus ordinary income tax on the entire $300,000 if you roll it into a traditional IRA and withdraw later.

### Mistake #4: Rolling Over to a High-Fee IRA

Moving from a mediocre 401(k) to a high-fee IRA — particularly one sold by a commission-based insurance agent or broker pushing loaded mutual funds or variable annuities — can leave you worse off than staying put. The Department of Labor has estimated that conflicted retirement advice costs Americans roughly $17 billion per year.

Stick with low-cost custodians charging zero account fees and offering broad access to low-expense-ratio index funds.

### Mistake #5: Not Considering Creditor Protection Differences

Federal law (ERISA) provides virtually unlimited creditor protection for 401(k) assets. IRA creditor protection varies by state. In most states, IRAs are protected in bankruptcy (up to roughly $1.5 million as of 2026 under federal bankruptcy exemptions), but protection from non-bankruptcy creditors — lawsuits, judgments, divorce — varies significantly. If you're in a high-liability profession (physician, business owner), consult an attorney before rolling over.

## Special Situations: What About After-Tax Contributions?

Many 401(k) plans allow after-tax (non-Roth) contributions beyond the standard $23,500 employee deferral limit for 2026. If your plan permits the mega backdoor Roth strategy, you may have both pre-tax and after-tax money in your 401(k).

Under current IRS rules, you can split the rollover:

- **Pre-tax contributions and all earnings → Traditional IRA**
- **After-tax contributions → Roth IRA** (this is the mega backdoor Roth in action)

This is done through a single direct rollover request, with the plan administrator splitting the check into two portions. Make sure both rollovers are direct (trustee-to-trustee) to avoid withholding complications.

## When NOT to Roll Over Your 401(k)

A rollover isn't always the right move. Consider keeping your money in the old 401(k) if:

- **The plan has institutional-class funds** with expense ratios below 0.03% that you can't access in an IRA
- **You're between 55 and 59½ and separated from service** — 401(k) plans allow penalty-free withdrawals after age 55 (the "Rule of 55"), while IRA withdrawals before 59½ generally trigger the 10% penalty unless you use 72(t) SEPP distributions
- **You need creditor protection** and your state's IRA protection is weaker than federal ERISA protections
- **You're still working past 73** and your current employer's 401(k) plan allows you to delay Required Minimum Distributions (the still-working exception doesn't apply to IRAs or old employer plans)

## The Bottom Line

A 401(k) to IRA rollover is one of the most common financial transactions in America — and one of the most consequential. The difference between a direct rollover executed properly and an indirect rollover gone wrong can easily cost $10,000 to $50,000 or more in unnecessary taxes and penalties.

The playbook is straightforward: **always choose a direct (trustee-to-trustee) rollover**, open your IRA at a low-cost custodian before initiating the transfer, reinvest immediately upon receipt, and verify your 1099-R coding. If you hold appreciated company stock, run the NUA numbers before rolling over. And if you're between 55 and 59½, make sure you understand the Rule of 55 before giving up penalty-free access to your 401(k) funds.

The money you save by getting this right isn't marginal — it compounds for decades. Treat the rollover with the seriousness it deserves.
