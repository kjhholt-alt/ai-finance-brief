## The Retirement Risk Nobody Wants to Talk About

Most retirement planning conversations focus on investment returns, withdrawal rates, and Social Security optimization. Those are important. But they all assume your savings will be spent on the retirement you've planned — travel, hobbies, time with family, a comfortable daily life. What they rarely account for is the single largest financial risk retirees face: the cost of long-term care.

The numbers are stark. According to the Department of Health and Human Services, roughly 70% of Americans turning 65 today will need some form of long-term care during their remaining years. The Genworth Cost of Care Survey for 2026 puts the national median cost of a private room in a nursing home at over $115,000 per year. A home health aide runs roughly $75,000 annually for full-time care. Assisted living facilities average around $65,000 per year. And the average duration of care needed is approximately three years — though roughly 20% of people who need care will require it for five years or more.

Do the math on a three-year nursing home stay and you're looking at $345,000 or more. A five-year stay exceeds half a million dollars. For most retirees, that's a substantial portion — or all — of their life savings. And Medicare doesn't cover it. Most people don't realize this until it's too late.

This guide breaks down your options for protecting against long-term care costs, the tradeoffs of each approach, and how to build a strategy that fits your financial situation and risk tolerance.

---

## Why Medicare Won't Save You

The most dangerous misconception in retirement planning is the belief that Medicare covers long-term care. It doesn't — at least not in any meaningful way.

Medicare covers up to 100 days of skilled nursing facility care after a qualifying hospital stay of at least three days. The first 20 days are fully covered. Days 21 through 100 require a daily copayment of $204.50 in 2026. After day 100, coverage ends entirely. And this only applies to skilled nursing care — not custodial care, which is what most long-term care actually involves. Custodial care means help with activities of daily living like bathing, dressing, eating, and moving around. That's what costs $75,000 to $115,000 per year, and Medicare pays zero toward it.

Medicaid does cover long-term care, but it's a means-tested program. You essentially need to spend down nearly all of your assets to qualify — in most states, the limit is $2,000 in countable assets for the individual receiving care. There are spousal protections that allow the healthy spouse to retain more, but the process is financially devastating for most middle-class families.

This gap — between what Medicare covers and what Medicaid requires — is where long-term care planning lives.

## Option 1: Traditional Long-Term Care Insurance

Traditional LTC insurance policies pay a daily or monthly benefit when you can no longer perform two or more activities of daily living, or when you have a cognitive impairment requiring supervision. You choose a daily benefit amount, a benefit period, an elimination period (the waiting period before benefits begin), and optional inflation protection.

### How It Works

A typical policy might provide $200 per day in benefits, a three-year benefit period, a 90-day elimination period, and 3% compound inflation protection. At age 55, this policy might cost $2,500 to $4,000 per year for a single person, or $3,500 to $5,500 for a couple.

The inflation protection rider is critical. Without it, a policy purchased at 55 with a $200 daily benefit will still pay $200 per day when you're 80 — but care costs will have roughly doubled. A 3% compound inflation rider means your $200 daily benefit grows to approximately $362 per day over 25 years, keeping pace with care cost increases.

### The Advantages

**Risk transfer.** You're shifting the catastrophic risk of a long care event to an insurance company. If you need five years of nursing home care, a well-structured policy could pay out $400,000 or more — far exceeding the premiums you paid.

**Asset protection.** Your retirement portfolio stays intact for your surviving spouse, your heirs, or your own non-care expenses.

**Medicaid partnership programs.** Most states now offer partnership-qualified LTC policies. If you exhaust your policy benefits, you can qualify for Medicaid while retaining assets equal to the benefits the policy paid out. This is a significant protection that traditional Medicaid doesn't offer.

### The Disadvantages

**Premium increases.** This is the elephant in the room. Insurers in the 2000s and 2010s dramatically underestimated claim rates and overestimated lapse rates. The result: massive premium increases on existing policyholders, sometimes 40% to 100% or more over the life of the policy. While regulatory oversight has improved and newer policies are priced more conservatively, the history of premium increases has eroded trust in the product.

**Use it or lose it.** If you never need long-term care — and roughly 30% of people won't — you've paid premiums for decades with nothing to show for it. Unlike life insurance, there's no death benefit. Your premiums are simply gone.

**Shrinking market.** Many major insurers have exited the standalone LTC market. Fewer carriers means less competition and potentially less pricing discipline. As of 2026, the number of companies actively selling new standalone LTC policies is a fraction of what it was 15 years ago.

**Underwriting.** If you wait too long or have significant health issues, you may not qualify. The ideal window for purchasing is typically between ages 50 and 65. After 70, coverage becomes extremely expensive if available at all.

### Who It's Best For

Traditional LTC insurance makes the most sense for people with moderate to substantial retirement savings — enough that they have meaningful assets to protect, but not so much that they could comfortably self-fund a multi-year care event. The sweet spot is typically households with $500,000 to $2 million in retirement assets.

## Option 2: Hybrid Life Insurance / LTC Policies

Hybrid policies — also called combination or linked-benefit policies — combine life insurance with long-term care coverage. They've become the dominant product in the LTC market over the past decade, now outselling standalone LTC policies by roughly four to one.

### How They Work

You pay a large single premium or a series of premiums over 5 to 10 years into a permanent life insurance policy. If you need long-term care, the policy accelerates your death benefit to pay for care. Many policies also include an extension-of-benefits rider that continues paying for care after the death benefit is exhausted — often doubling or tripling the total LTC benefit pool.

For example, you might pay a $150,000 single premium for a policy with a $200,000 death benefit. If you need care, the policy pays LTC benefits from that $200,000 pool. With a 2x extension rider, your total available LTC benefit pool could be $400,000. If you never need care, your beneficiaries receive the $200,000 death benefit. And if you change your mind entirely, most policies offer a return-of-premium feature that gives you back most or all of your premium.

### The Advantages

**No "use it or lose it" problem.** This is the biggest selling point. Your money goes somewhere regardless: to LTC benefits, a death benefit, or back to you as a return of premium. You're never paying for nothing.

**Premium guarantees.** Unlike standalone LTC policies, hybrid policy premiums are typically guaranteed — no surprises 15 years in. The insurance company takes on the investment and mortality risk, not you.

**Simplified underwriting.** Hybrid policies generally have somewhat more lenient underwriting than standalone LTC policies, making them accessible to a slightly broader health profile.

**Tax advantages.** Under the Pension Protection Act, LTC benefits paid from a hybrid policy are generally income-tax-free. The death benefit passes to beneficiaries income-tax-free under standard life insurance rules. And if the policy is funded with after-tax dollars (which it usually is), there's no additional tax on the LTC withdrawals.

### The Disadvantages

**Large upfront cost.** The single-premium model typically requires $75,000 to $250,000 upfront. Even multi-pay options involve substantial annual premiums. This is a significant liquidity event that redirects funds from your investment portfolio.

**Opportunity cost.** That $150,000 single premium, invested in a diversified portfolio earning 7% annually, would grow to approximately $580,000 over 20 years. You're giving up substantial growth potential in exchange for the insurance protection. For younger purchasers with long time horizons, this opportunity cost is significant.

**Lower LTC benefit relative to standalone.** Dollar for dollar, a hybrid policy typically provides less LTC coverage than a standalone policy for the same cost. The tradeoff for the "money back" guarantee is a smaller insurance component.

**Inflation protection limitations.** While hybrid policies offer inflation riders, they're often less generous than what's available on standalone LTC policies. Some offer simple rather than compound inflation growth, which makes a meaningful difference over 20 to 30 years.

### Who It's Best For

Hybrid policies work well for people who have significant liquid assets they're willing to reposition, want guaranteed premiums, and can't stomach the "use it or lose it" nature of standalone LTC insurance. They're also a fit for people who want to combine their life insurance and LTC planning into a single product. Typical buyers have $1 million or more in investable assets and are willing to allocate $100,000 to $200,000 to this strategy.

## Option 3: Self-Funding (Self-Insuring)

Self-funding means setting aside a dedicated pool of assets to pay for potential long-term care costs rather than transferring the risk to an insurer. This isn't the same as ignoring the risk — it's a deliberate strategy with its own logic.

### How to Structure Self-Funding

The responsible version of self-funding involves earmarking a specific portion of your portfolio for potential care costs. This isn't money you count in your retirement spending projections — it's a dedicated reserve.

**Calculate your target.** A reasonable self-funding target is three to four years of care at the cost level appropriate for your area, adjusted for inflation to your expected age of need. If you're 60 and expect potential care needs might begin around age 82, and current nursing home costs in your area are $120,000 per year, you need to account for roughly 22 years of inflation. At 3% annual healthcare inflation, that $120,000 becomes approximately $230,000 per year. Three years at that cost is $690,000.

**Invest the reserve appropriately.** The care reserve should be invested more conservatively than your general retirement portfolio, but not so conservatively that it can't keep pace with healthcare inflation. A balanced allocation of 40% to 50% equities and 50% to 60% bonds within the earmarked reserve is reasonable for someone 15 or more years from expected need. As you approach the age range where care becomes more likely, shift toward greater liquidity.

**Review annually.** Reassess the adequacy of your self-funding reserve annually. As care costs change, your health changes, and your overall financial situation evolves, the reserve target should be adjusted.

### The Advantages

**Full control.** You maintain complete control over your money. No premiums to an insurance company, no policy terms to navigate, no claims process.

**No premium risk.** You're not exposed to the premium increase risk that has plagued standalone LTC policyholders.

**If you don't need care, you keep everything.** The earmarked funds become part of your estate or your spending money. Unlike insurance premiums, nothing is "lost."

**Investment returns.** Your self-funding reserve can earn market returns over the accumulation period, potentially growing significantly before you need it.

### The Disadvantages

**Tail risk exposure.** Self-funding works well for average care scenarios (two to three years). It fails catastrophically for worst-case scenarios — a 10-year Alzheimer's journey, for example, could cost over $1 million. Insurance exists precisely to cover these tail risks that self-funding cannot.

**Requires substantial assets.** To responsibly self-fund, you need a large enough portfolio that earmarking $500,000 to $700,000 for potential care doesn't compromise your other retirement goals. This generally means total investable assets of $2.5 million or more.

**Behavioral risk.** It's psychologically difficult to leave a large sum of money untouched for a contingency that may never happen. The temptation to "borrow" from the care reserve for other purposes is real.

**Spousal impact.** If one spouse needs extended care and self-funding is depleted, the surviving spouse's financial security is directly threatened.

### Who It's Best For

Self-funding is appropriate for high-net-worth households — generally those with $3 million or more in investable assets — who can absorb even a worst-case care scenario without devastating their surviving spouse's financial security or their estate plan.

## Option 4: Medicaid Planning

Medicaid planning involves structuring your assets and income to qualify for Medicaid coverage of long-term care while preserving as much wealth as possible for your spouse and heirs. This is a legitimate legal strategy, but it must be done correctly and well in advance of any care need.

### The Five-Year Lookback

Medicaid's most important rule for planning purposes is the five-year lookback period. When you apply for Medicaid, the state examines all asset transfers made in the previous five years (60 months). Any gifts or transfers made during this period for less than fair market value result in a penalty period — a stretch of time during which you're ineligible for Medicaid coverage despite otherwise qualifying.

This means Medicaid planning must begin at least five years before you might need care. Given that the average age of nursing home admission is around 83, and planning should account for early-onset scenarios, beginning this planning in your early-to-mid 70s is prudent.

### Common Medicaid Planning Strategies

**Irrevocable trusts.** Transferring assets into an irrevocable trust more than five years before applying for Medicaid removes those assets from your countable estate. You can't be the trustee or retain control, and you generally can't be a beneficiary during your lifetime. But the assets are preserved for your heirs.

**Spousal protections.** The Community Spouse Resource Allowance (CSRA) lets the healthy spouse retain a significant portion of joint assets — up to $154,140 in 2026 in most states — plus a monthly income allowance. Strategic titling and income allocation can maximize these protections.

**Caregiver child exemption.** If an adult child lives in the parent's home and provides care for at least two years before the parent enters a nursing home, the home can be transferred to that child without triggering a Medicaid penalty.

### Important Cautions

Medicaid planning is not a DIY project. The rules are complex, vary significantly by state, and mistakes can result in extended periods of Medicaid ineligibility. An elder law attorney specializing in Medicaid planning is essential.

Additionally, Medicaid estate recovery programs allow states to seek reimbursement for Medicaid costs from the deceased beneficiary's estate. Proper planning must account for this.

## Building Your Long-Term Care Strategy: A Decision Framework

The right approach depends on your financial situation, health, family history, and risk tolerance. Here's a practical framework:

### If Your Investable Assets Are Under $500,000

Your primary strategy is likely Medicaid planning combined with whatever assets you can protect for your spouse. Consider consulting an elder law attorney in your state to understand available protections. A standalone LTC policy with modest benefits could provide a bridge, particularly through a Medicaid partnership program.

### If Your Investable Assets Are $500,000 to $1.5 Million

This is the zone where insurance provides the most value. You have meaningful assets to protect, but not enough to comfortably self-fund a multi-year care event. Consider either a standalone LTC policy (if premiums are manageable within your budget) or a hybrid policy (if you have $100,000 to $150,000 you can reposition from conservative holdings). A Medicaid partnership policy adds an extra safety net for worst-case scenarios.

### If Your Investable Assets Are $1.5 Million to $3 Million

You have more options. A hybrid policy funded with a portion of your conservative allocation gives you guaranteed coverage without the premium increase risk. Alternatively, a combination of partial self-funding (earmarking 18 to 24 months of care costs) plus a standalone or hybrid policy covering the remainder can reduce premium costs while still addressing tail risk.

### If Your Investable Assets Exceed $3 Million

Self-funding becomes a viable primary strategy. You can absorb even an extended care scenario without threatening your surviving spouse's security. However, even wealthy families sometimes add a hybrid policy as a capital-efficient way to handle care costs — the leverage of insurance means $150,000 in premium can produce $400,000 or more in benefits, preserving portfolio assets for heirs.

## Action Steps to Take Right Now

Regardless of your financial situation, these steps will put you in a stronger position:

**1. Estimate your exposure.** Look up the cost of care in your area using the Genworth Cost of Care Survey. Calculate what three years and five years of care would cost at current prices, then inflate those numbers to your expected age of need.

**2. Review your current coverage.** Check whether your employer offers group LTC benefits — these sometimes come with simplified underwriting and subsidized rates. Review any existing life insurance policies for LTC riders you may not be using.

**3. Talk to a fee-only financial planner.** Specifically, one with experience in long-term care planning. Commission-based advisors have an incentive to sell you a product. Fee-only planners can evaluate whether insurance, self-funding, or a combination makes the most sense for your situation.

**4. Consider your health trajectory.** If you have a family history of Alzheimer's, dementia, or other conditions that commonly require extended care, your risk profile is elevated. This tilts the analysis toward more insurance coverage rather than less.

**5. Don't wait too long.** The optimal window for purchasing LTC insurance — standalone or hybrid — is between ages 50 and 65. Premiums increase sharply after 65, and underwriting becomes more restrictive. Health events can make you uninsurable. If you're in this window, get quotes now even if you don't buy immediately.

**6. Coordinate with your estate plan.** Long-term care planning doesn't exist in isolation. Your approach should integrate with your estate plan, tax strategy, and overall retirement income plan. An irrevocable trust used for Medicaid planning, for example, has implications for your estate plan and gift tax strategy.

## The Bottom Line

Long-term care is the retirement risk that destroys the best-laid plans. A 70% probability of needing some form of care, combined with costs that can easily exceed $300,000, makes this a risk that demands a deliberate strategy — not hope.

The good news is that you have real options. Whether you transfer the risk through insurance, self-fund with a dedicated reserve, plan strategically for Medicaid eligibility, or combine multiple approaches, the key is making a conscious choice before the need arises. The worst strategy is no strategy at all — and that's exactly what most Americans have today.

Start the conversation with your financial advisor now. Your future self — and your family — will thank you.
