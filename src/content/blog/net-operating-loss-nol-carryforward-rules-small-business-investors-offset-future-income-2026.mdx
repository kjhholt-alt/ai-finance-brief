## Net Operating Loss (NOL) Carryforward Rules: How to Turn Business Losses Into Future Tax Savings in 2026

Nobody starts a business planning to lose money. But losses happen — a rough launch year, an economic downturn, a major capital investment that temporarily pushes expenses far above revenue. The question isn't whether you'll ever have a loss year. The question is whether you'll know how to use it.

The net operating loss (NOL) carryforward is one of the most powerful — and most overlooked — provisions in the tax code. It allows you to take a loss from one year and apply it against taxable income in future years, effectively reducing your tax bill when profits return. For small business owners, real estate investors, and anyone with volatile income, understanding NOL rules isn't optional. It's the difference between paying tens of thousands more in taxes than you need to and keeping that money working in your business.

The rules have changed significantly since the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) of 2017, and with several TCJA provisions set to evolve, 2026 is a pivotal year. Here's exactly how NOL carryforwards work, what limitations you need to know, and how to maximize their value.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **A net operating loss occurs when your allowable tax deductions exceed your gross income** for the year. You can carry that loss forward indefinitely to offset future taxable income.
- **Post-TCJA rules limit NOL deductions to 80% of taxable income** in any given year — you can no longer wipe out your entire tax bill with carryforward losses.
- **NOL carrybacks were eliminated for most taxpayers** after 2020, meaning losses can only go forward, not backward (with narrow exceptions for farming and insurance).
- **The excess business loss limitation ($305,000 single / $610,000 married filing jointly in 2026)** caps how much business loss you can deduct in a single year — anything above converts to an NOL carryforward automatically.
- **Strategic timing of income and deductions** can dramatically increase the value of your NOL carryforwards, especially in years when your marginal tax rate is highest.

## What Exactly Is a Net Operating Loss?

A net operating loss happens when your total allowable deductions exceed your total income for the tax year. But not every deduction counts toward the NOL calculation. Under Section 172 of the Internal Revenue Code, specific modifications apply.

### What's Included in the NOL Calculation

| Included | Excluded |
|----------|----------|
| Business expenses (Schedule C) | Capital losses in excess of capital gains |
| Rental property losses (if you qualify) | Personal exemption deductions |
| Partnership/S-corp pass-through losses | Non-business deductions exceeding non-business income |
| Farm losses | Section 1202 QSBS exclusion amounts |
| Casualty and theft losses from business property | Domestic production activities deduction |

The critical distinction: an NOL is fundamentally a *business* concept. You can't generate an NOL purely from personal deductions like the standard deduction or itemized deductions that aren't connected to a trade or business. Your wage income, business income, and business deductions flow into the calculation. Personal deductions only count to the extent they don't exceed non-business income.

### A Simple Example

Sarah runs a consulting business and also earns $40,000 from a part-time W-2 job. In 2026, her business has a terrible year:

- **W-2 income:** $40,000
- **Business revenue:** $25,000
- **Business expenses:** $120,000
- **Business loss:** ($95,000)
- **Combined income:** $40,000 + $25,000 - $120,000 = **($55,000)**

After applying the NOL modification rules (removing non-business deductions that exceed non-business income), Sarah has an approximate NOL of **$55,000** that she can carry forward to 2027 and beyond.

## The 80% Taxable Income Limitation: Why You Can't Zero Out Your Tax Bill

Before the TCJA, NOL carryforwards could offset 100% of your taxable income. Those days are gone.

Under current rules, NOL deductions are limited to **80% of taxable income** (computed without regard to the NOL deduction itself). This means even with massive carryforward losses, you'll always owe taxes on at least 20% of your income in profitable years.

### How the 80% Rule Works in Practice

Let's say you have a $200,000 NOL carryforward and earn $150,000 of taxable income in 2027:

| Item | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Taxable income (before NOL deduction) | $150,000 |
| Maximum NOL deduction (80% × $150,000) | $120,000 |
| Taxable income after NOL deduction | $30,000 |
| Remaining NOL carryforward | $80,000 |

Without the 80% limitation, you'd owe zero tax. Instead, you owe tax on $30,000. The remaining $80,000 carries forward to 2028 and beyond — there's no expiration date.

**The silver lining:** the indefinite carryforward period means your losses never expire. Under old rules (pre-TCJA), NOLs could carry forward only 20 years. If your business had a massive loss, the clock was ticking. Now, that $80,000 sits patiently waiting until you have income to offset, whether that's next year or 15 years from now.

## The Excess Business Loss Limitation: A Trap for High-Earning Business Owners

Before your NOL even gets calculated, another limitation kicks in — the **excess business loss (EBL) limitation** under Section 461(l). This is the speed bump most small business owners don't see coming.

For 2026, the EBL threshold is approximately **$305,000 for single filers** and **$610,000 for married filing jointly**. Any aggregate business loss exceeding this threshold is disallowed for the current year and automatically converted into an NOL carryforward.

### Example: How the EBL Limitation Creates Forced NOL Carryforwards

Mark and Lisa are married filing jointly. Mark earns $400,000 as a surgeon. Lisa runs a startup that lost $900,000 in 2026.

- **Combined business loss:** ($900,000) business loss + $400,000 W-2 income = ($500,000) net
- **But wait** — the EBL limitation caps their deductible business loss at $610,000
- **Lisa's loss exceeds that:** $900,000 - $610,000 = $290,000 disallowed
- **That $290,000 becomes an NOL carryforward** to 2027

The EBL limitation was originally set to expire after 2025, but has been extended through 2028. This is a critical planning point: even if your business hemorrhages money, there's a ceiling on how much you can deduct against other income in any single year.

## NOL Carryback Rules: Mostly Gone, With Exceptions

The TCJA eliminated NOL carrybacks for most taxpayers. Before 2018, you could carry an NOL back two years to claim a refund of taxes already paid. That option no longer exists for general business NOLs.

### The Exceptions

**Farming losses** can still be carried back two years. If you operate a farming business (as defined under Section 263A), you retain the carryback option. This includes crop production, livestock raising, and certain agricultural processing operations.

**Insurance companies** other than life insurance companies can also carry back NOLs two years.

Everyone else carries forward only. This makes NOL planning more important than ever — you can't retroactively fix a bad year by claiming a refund, so you need to plan ahead for how and when to use your carryforward losses.

## Five Strategies to Maximize Your NOL Carryforwards

### 1. Accelerate Income Into High-NOL Years

If you have a substantial NOL carryforward, consider accelerating income into years where you can use it. This might mean:

- Taking a larger distribution from a Roth conversion (since conversion income can be offset by NOLs)
- Invoicing clients in December instead of January
- Exercising stock options in a year when you have carryforward losses to absorb the ordinary income
- Selling appreciated assets to realize capital gains that your NOL can partially offset

The 80% limitation means you'll still owe some tax, but using your NOL against income that would otherwise be taxed at 32% or 37% is far more valuable than using it against income in the 12% bracket.

### 2. Stack Deductions in Loss Years, Defer Them From Profitable Years

If your business is already in a loss position, additional deductions in that year simply increase your NOL carryforward — they don't provide an immediate tax benefit. Consider whether some discretionary deductions (like bonus depreciation on equipment purchases) might be more valuable in a profitable year.

Conversely, if you're approaching a loss year, consider accelerating deductible expenses into that year to build the largest possible NOL carryforward for use in high-income future years.

### 3. Monitor the Excess Business Loss Limitation

Keep a running tally of your aggregate business losses throughout the year. If you're approaching the $305,000/$610,000 threshold, understand that additional losses won't reduce your current-year tax — they'll convert to NOL carryforwards subject to the 80% limitation in future years. This might change your timing decisions on when to incur certain expenses.

### 4. Coordinate NOLs With State Tax Rules

State conformity to federal NOL rules varies enormously. Some key differences:

| State Approach | Examples | Key Difference |
|---------------|----------|----------------|
| Full conformity | Most states | Follow federal 80% rule and indefinite carryforward |
| Limited carryforward | Pennsylvania | May limit carryforward to a set number of years |
| Different percentage cap | Some states | May limit to less than 80% of taxable income |
| No NOL deduction | A handful of states | Don't allow NOL carryforwards at all |

If you operate in multiple states or are considering relocation, state NOL treatment should factor into your decision. A state that conforms to federal rules could save you thousands compared to one that caps or eliminates NOL carryforwards.

### 5. Keep Impeccable Records

NOLs can carry forward indefinitely, which means the IRS might question a loss from 2026 when you use it in 2033. You need to maintain:

- Complete tax returns for every year an NOL was generated
- Documentation of the NOL computation and modification calculations
- Records showing the carryforward balance each year
- All supporting business records (receipts, financial statements, bank records)

The burden of proof is on you to substantiate both the original loss and the carryforward amount. Poor recordkeeping is the number one reason taxpayers lose NOL deductions in audits.

## Special Situations: NOLs and Pass-Through Entities

If you own an S-corporation or partnership, business losses pass through to your personal return. But several additional limitations apply *before* the NOL rules:

1. **Basis limitation** — You can only deduct losses up to your basis in the entity
2. **At-risk limitation** — Losses are limited to amounts you're economically at risk for
3. **Passive activity limitation** — If you don't materially participate, losses may be suspended as passive losses (not NOLs)
4. **Excess business loss limitation** — The EBL rules described above
5. **NOL rules** — Only after passing through all four gates above

Each limitation applies in order. A loss that gets trapped at the passive activity level, for example, never becomes an NOL — it's a suspended passive loss with its own separate rules. Understanding where your loss gets caught in this hierarchy determines which planning strategies apply.

## NOLs and the Alternative Minimum Tax (AMT)

For AMT purposes, the NOL deduction is calculated separately as an "AMTNOL." The limitation is also 80% of alternative minimum taxable income. Importantly, some deductions allowed for regular tax NOL purposes may not be allowed for AMTNOL purposes. If you're subject to AMT in the year you're using your NOL carryforward, your actual tax benefit may be lower than expected.

Work with a tax professional to model both regular tax and AMT scenarios when planning NOL utilization.

---

## What Happens to NOLs When You Sell or Close Your Business

If you close your sole proprietorship, your NOL carryforwards survive. They're personal deductions that remain on your individual return regardless of whether the business that generated them still exists.

For S-corporations and partnerships, however, the rules are more nuanced. Losses that were suspended at the entity level (due to basis, at-risk, or passive activity rules) may become deductible in the year you dispose of your entire interest — this is a common planning opportunity that owners miss.

If you're planning to sell or wind down a business with accumulated losses, consult a tax advisor *before* the transaction to ensure you capture the maximum deduction.

## The Bottom Line: Your Losses Are an Asset

A net operating loss isn't just a bad year on paper — it's a future tax deduction with real dollar value. At a 37% marginal rate, a $100,000 NOL carryforward is worth up to $29,600 in future tax savings (accounting for the 80% limitation). At 24%, it's still worth $19,200.

The key is understanding the rules well enough to time your income and deductions strategically, maintain proper documentation, and avoid the traps (like the excess business loss limitation) that can reduce or defer the benefit.

If you had a loss year in 2025 or are headed toward one in 2026, don't just file your return and move on. Calculate your NOL, document it thoroughly, and build a multi-year plan to use it when it delivers the most value. The tax code may not hand you many consolation prizes — but the NOL carryforward is one of the best.
