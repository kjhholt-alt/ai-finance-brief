## The Pension Decision That Can Make or Break Your Retirement

If you're approaching retirement with a defined benefit pension, you're facing one of the most consequential financial decisions of your life — and it's irreversible. Take the lump sum, and there's no going back. Choose the monthly annuity, and you've locked in a payment stream that can't be adjusted if your circumstances change.

According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, approximately 15% of private-sector workers and 86% of state and local government employees still participate in defined benefit pension plans. For millions of these workers, the "lump sum vs. annuity" decision involves hundreds of thousands — sometimes millions — of dollars. And yet most people make this choice based on gut feeling, a coworker's anecdote, or a financial advisor's recommendation without understanding the math behind it.

The right answer depends on your specific numbers: interest rates, your health and life expectancy, other income sources, tax situation, and how you'd actually invest a lump sum. In 2026, with interest rates elevated compared to the near-zero era of 2020–2022, the calculus has shifted meaningfully — and not in the direction most people assume.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Higher interest rates in 2026 mean smaller lump sum offers** — pension lump sums are calculated using discount rates tied to corporate bond yields, and the current rate environment produces lump sums that are 15–25% lower than what was offered in 2021.
- **The breakeven age typically falls between 80 and 85** — if you expect to live past the breakeven point, the annuity usually delivers more total lifetime income.
- **A lump sum gives you control, flexibility, and a bequest** — you can invest it, adjust withdrawals, and leave the remainder to heirs, but you also bear the investment risk and longevity risk.
- **The annuity eliminates longevity risk** — you cannot outlive a pension payment, which makes it especially valuable if you have longevity in your family or limited other guaranteed income.
- **Tax treatment differs significantly** — a lump sum rolled into an IRA defers taxes and gives you withdrawal flexibility, while annuity payments are taxed as ordinary income in every year you receive them.

## How Pension Lump Sums Are Calculated

Understanding the math behind your lump sum offer is critical because it reveals whether the employer is giving you a fair deal or a discounted one.

A pension lump sum is the present value of your future annuity payments, discounted using interest rates specified by the IRS. These rates, called segment rates, are derived from corporate bond yields and are published monthly. The IRS uses three segments:

- **Segment 1** (payments in years 1–5): based on short-term corporate bond yields
- **Segment 2** (payments in years 6–20): based on medium-term yields
- **Segment 3** (payments beyond year 20): based on long-term yields

When interest rates are low, future payments are worth more today, producing a larger lump sum. When rates rise — as they have since 2022 — the lump sum shrinks because each future dollar is discounted more heavily.

### The 2026 Rate Impact

In October 2024, the IRS segment rates averaged approximately 5.0%, 5.3%, and 5.5% across the three segments. Compare that to October 2021, when they were 0.8%, 2.8%, and 3.4%. That shift alone reduced typical lump sum offers by 20–30% for someone with a 25-year life expectancy.

Here's what that looks like in practice:

| Monthly Pension | Life Expectancy | Lump Sum at 2021 Rates | Lump Sum at 2026 Rates |
|-----------------|-----------------|------------------------|------------------------|
| $3,000/month | 25 years | $742,000 | $548,000 |
| $5,000/month | 25 years | $1,237,000 | $913,000 |
| $8,000/month | 20 years | $1,632,000 | $1,264,000 |

If you were offered a lump sum in 2021 and passed on it, the same pension benefit now produces a significantly smaller check. This doesn't mean the lump sum is a bad deal — it means you need to evaluate it against the current alternatives, not against what was available years ago.

## The Breakeven Analysis: When the Annuity Wins

The most important calculation in the pension decision is the breakeven age — the point at which the total annuity payments received equal the value of the lump sum plus its investment returns.

### How to Calculate Your Breakeven

1. **Start with your lump sum offer** (let's use $550,000).
2. **Assume an investment return** on the lump sum if you roll it into an IRA. A balanced 60/40 portfolio has historically returned about 7–8% annually before inflation, but a more conservative 5–6% assumption is appropriate for retirement planning.
3. **Assume a withdrawal rate** from the IRA — typically 4–5% per year.
4. **Compare that annual withdrawal to your annual pension payment.**

### Worked Example

- **Pension offer:** $3,200/month ($38,400/year)
- **Lump sum offer:** $550,000
- **Assumed IRA return:** 6% annually
- **Assumed withdrawal:** 4% of portfolio ($22,000 in year one, growing with portfolio)

At a 4% withdrawal rate, the lump sum generates only $22,000 per year initially — far less than the $38,400 annual pension. You'd need to withdraw 7% annually to match the pension payment, which depletes the portfolio faster and risks running out of money.

**Breakeven age in this scenario:** approximately 82. If you retire at 62, you'd need to live past 82 for the annuity to deliver more total value. If you retire at 65, the breakeven drops to around 84.

### What the Actuarial Data Says

According to the Society of Actuaries' mortality tables, a 65-year-old male has a 50% chance of living to 85 and a 25% chance of reaching 90. A 65-year-old female has a 50% chance of living to 87 and a 25% chance of reaching 92. For a married couple both aged 65, there's a 50% probability that at least one spouse lives to 92.

These numbers tilt the math toward the annuity for most retirees, especially those in good health. The annuity is essentially a bet that you'll live a long time — and statistically, that's a bet most people should take.

## When the Lump Sum Makes More Sense

Despite the actuarial argument for the annuity, there are several situations where the lump sum is clearly the better choice.

### 1. You Have Health Issues or a Shortened Life Expectancy

If your doctor has told you to get your affairs in order, or your family history shows consistently shorter lifespans, the breakeven analysis shifts dramatically. Dying at 75 with a lump sum means your heirs inherit the remaining IRA balance. Dying at 75 with an annuity means the payments stop (unless you elected a survivor benefit, which reduces your monthly payment).

### 2. Your Pension Plan's Sponsor Is Financially Shaky

This matters more than most people realize. Private-sector pensions are insured by the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation (PBGC), but the PBGC has maximum guarantee limits. In 2026, the maximum guaranteed annual benefit for someone retiring at 65 is approximately $81,000. If your pension exceeds that amount and your employer goes bankrupt, you'd lose the excess.

Check your plan's funded status in its annual funding notice (required under ERISA). A plan funded below 80% should raise concerns. Below 60% should trigger serious consideration of the lump sum.

### 3. You Want Control Over Your Investment Strategy

The annuity locks you into a fixed payment. A lump sum rolled into an IRA gives you the ability to:

- **Adjust withdrawals** based on market conditions (take less in down years, more in good years)
- **Invest tax-efficiently** using asset location strategies, tax-loss harvesting, and Roth conversions
- **Access the principal** for large expenses (medical emergencies, helping a child buy a home)
- **Leave a legacy** to heirs, who receive the remaining IRA balance through the inherited IRA rules

### 4. You Have Substantial Other Guaranteed Income

If you and your spouse will collect combined Social Security benefits of $60,000–$80,000 per year, and you have additional income from rental properties or a part-time consulting practice, you may already have enough guaranteed income to cover essential expenses. In that case, the lump sum's investment flexibility adds more value than another guaranteed income stream.

### 5. The Lump Sum Offer Is Generous Relative to the Annuity

Some employers — particularly those trying to de-risk their pension obligations — offer lump sums that exceed the actuarial present value of the annuity. Run the breakeven calculation. If the breakeven age is 90+, the lump sum is essentially priced in your favor.

## When the Annuity Is the Clear Winner

### 1. You're Healthy With Longevity in Your Family

If your parents and grandparents routinely lived into their late 80s and 90s, the annuity's value increases substantially. Every year past the breakeven age is "free money" — income the lump sum would have run out of.

### 2. You're Risk-Averse or Uncomfortable Investing

This sounds like a minor psychological factor, but it's one of the most predictive. Studies from the Employee Benefit Research Institute (EBRI) consistently find that retirees who take lump sums and manage them independently spend them down 30–40% faster than optimal withdrawal models predict. Behavioral factors — panic selling, overspending, bad advisor recommendations — erode lump sums in practice even when the math works in theory.

If you know you'll struggle to maintain investment discipline during a 30–40% market drawdown, the annuity removes that risk entirely.

### 3. Your Spouse Depends on the Income

The joint-and-survivor annuity option (typically 50%, 75%, or 100% survivor benefit) guarantees income for your spouse's lifetime as well. Yes, the monthly payment is reduced — a 100% survivor option typically pays 10–15% less than the single-life annuity — but it provides insurance against the scenario where you die first and your spouse needs decades more income.

### 4. Interest Rates Have Made the Lump Sum Unfavorable

In the current elevated-rate environment, lump sum offers are mathematically less attractive. When your employer's discount rate is 5.5% but you'd realistically earn 6–7% on a balanced portfolio, the spread is too thin to justify taking on investment and longevity risk. In 2020–2021, when lump sums were calculated at 2–3% rates, you were essentially getting a premium for taking the money. That premium has largely evaporated.

## Tax Implications: The Hidden Factor

The tax treatment of each option is often the swing factor that tips the decision.

### Lump Sum Rolled to a Traditional IRA

- **No immediate tax liability** — the rollover is tax-deferred
- **You control when and how much you withdraw**, managing your annual tax bracket
- **Enables Roth conversion strategies** — convert portions in low-income years before Social Security and RMDs kick in
- **RMDs begin at age 73** (or 75 if born in 1960 or later under SECURE 2.0), forcing taxable withdrawals whether you need them or not

### Monthly Annuity Payments

- **Each payment is taxed as ordinary income** in the year received
- **No flexibility** — you can't reduce the payment in a year when other income is high
- **Combined with Social Security**, pension income can push you into higher brackets and trigger Social Security benefit taxation (up to 85% of benefits become taxable)
- **May trigger IRMAA Medicare surcharges** if combined income exceeds thresholds ($103,000 for single filers, $206,000 for married filing jointly in 2026)

### The Roth Conversion Opportunity

This is where the lump sum gains a significant tax advantage. If you retire at 62 with a lump sum IRA rollover, you have a window of 3–11 years (depending on your Social Security claiming age) where your income may be relatively low. During this "gap period," you can convert portions of the traditional IRA to a Roth IRA at historically low tax rates.

For example, a married couple with no other income could convert approximately $94,000 per year and stay within the 12% federal tax bracket in 2026. Over eight years before Social Security begins, that's $752,000 moved to a Roth — growing tax-free for the rest of their lives, exempt from RMDs, and passed to heirs with no income tax liability (though the 10-year distribution rule applies).

This strategy is impossible with a pension annuity.

## The Hybrid Approach: Partial Lump Sum

Some pension plans offer a partial lump sum option — you take a reduced monthly annuity plus a one-time lump sum payment. This can be an ideal middle ground:

- **Guaranteed income** from the reduced annuity covers essential expenses
- **Lump sum portion** provides flexibility, investment growth potential, and a bequest
- **Reduced risk** compared to going all-in on either option

Not all plans offer this, but if yours does, it deserves serious consideration. The partial approach is particularly compelling when the breakeven analysis is close (breakeven age of 83–86) and you'd benefit from both guaranteed income and investment flexibility.

## A Decision Framework: Five Questions to Answer

Before making your choice, work through these five questions:

**1. What is your breakeven age?** Run the calculation with realistic return assumptions (5–6%, not 10%). If you'd need to live past 88 for the annuity to win, the lump sum is likely better. If the breakeven is 80, the annuity almost certainly wins.

**2. How funded is your pension plan?** Check the annual funding notice. Government plans are generally safer. Private plans below 80% funded status warrant extra scrutiny.

**3. What does your total retirement income picture look like?** Map out all income sources: Social Security, spouse's pension, rental income, part-time work, portfolio withdrawals. If you're already rich in guaranteed income, the lump sum's flexibility is more valuable. If the pension would be your primary guaranteed income, the annuity is harder to replace.

**4. What's your tax strategy?** If you have a Roth conversion plan or expect to manage your tax bracket actively, the lump sum enables strategies the annuity forecloses. If tax optimization isn't something you'll actively manage, this advantage evaporates.

**5. Can you honestly maintain investment discipline?** Be brutally honest. If a 35% market crash would cause you to sell everything and move to cash, you'll underperform the annuity regardless of what the spreadsheet says. The annuity's greatest value may be protecting you from yourself.

## What to Do Next

1. **Request your pension benefit statement** showing both the lump sum offer and the monthly annuity options (single life, joint 50%, joint 75%, joint 100%).
2. **Run the breakeven analysis** using a pension calculator or spreadsheet. Use 5.5–6.5% as your assumed investment return for the lump sum.
3. **Map your total retirement income** from all sources across both scenarios.
4. **Model the tax implications** of each option, paying special attention to Social Security taxation, IRMAA surcharges, and Roth conversion opportunities.
5. **Consult a fee-only fiduciary financial advisor** — not an advisor who earns commissions on annuity products or IRA rollovers. The pension decision is too large and too irreversible to make without professional analysis of your specific numbers.

## The Bottom Line

There is no universally correct answer to the pension lump sum vs. annuity question. The right choice depends on your health, your plan's financial stability, your tax situation, your other income sources, and your behavioral relationship with money.

What is universally true: the worst approach is making this decision quickly, emotionally, or based on what a coworker chose. The difference between the optimal and suboptimal choice can easily exceed $200,000 in lifetime value. Take the time to run the numbers, understand the tradeoffs, and make the decision that fits your specific retirement plan — not someone else's.
