## The Roth 401(k) vs Traditional 401(k) Decision Could Cost You Hundreds of Thousands of Dollars

Every year, millions of workers open their 401(k) enrollment portal, stare at the Roth vs. Traditional toggle, and guess. That guess — repeated over a 20- or 30-year career — can mean the difference between retiring with $1.5 million and retiring with $2 million or more in after-tax purchasing power.

The core question is deceptively simple: do you want to pay taxes on your contributions now (Roth) or later (Traditional)? But the right answer depends on a web of factors most people never think through — your current marginal tax rate, your expected retirement income, state tax exposure, Social Security taxation, Medicare IRMAA surcharges, and whether you're likely to inherit wealth or leave it to heirs.

Here's how to actually think through this decision with real numbers, not rules of thumb.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Traditional 401(k) contributions reduce your taxable income today** — you pay taxes when you withdraw in retirement at ordinary income rates.
- **Roth 401(k) contributions are made with after-tax dollars** — qualified withdrawals in retirement are completely tax-free, including all investment growth.
- **If your tax rate stays exactly the same**, Traditional and Roth produce identical after-tax results — the math is symmetric.
- **The real decision hinges on whether your marginal tax rate will be higher or lower in retirement** — and most people underestimate their future rate.
- **Starting in 2026, high earners over 50 must use Roth for catch-up contributions** under SECURE 2.0, making this decision partially mandatory for some workers.

---

## How Traditional 401(k) Contributions Work

When you contribute to a Traditional 401(k), your contribution comes out of your paycheck before federal income tax is calculated. If you earn $150,000 and contribute $23,500 (the 2026 limit), your taxable income drops to $126,500. At the 24% marginal bracket, that saves you roughly $5,640 in federal taxes this year.

Your money grows tax-deferred inside the account. You don't pay capital gains taxes when holdings are sold, and dividends aren't taxed as they're reinvested. The tax bill arrives when you withdraw funds in retirement — every dollar withdrawn is taxed as ordinary income.

**The key assumptions behind choosing Traditional:**

- You're in a higher tax bracket now than you expect to be in retirement
- You want to maximize your current take-home pay (or redirect tax savings to other investments)
- You believe tax rates will remain roughly stable or decrease
- You're comfortable with Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs) starting at age 73 (or 75 for those born in 1960 or later)

---

## How Roth 401(k) Contributions Work

Roth 401(k) contributions come from your after-tax income. Using the same $150,000 salary example, contributing $23,500 to a Roth 401(k) doesn't reduce your taxable income at all — you still pay taxes on the full $150,000. That's a real cost today.

But the payoff is significant: all qualified withdrawals in retirement — including decades of investment growth — come out completely tax-free. A $23,500 annual contribution that grows to $800,000 over 25 years means $800,000 of tax-free income in retirement. Not one penny goes to the IRS.

Unlike Roth IRAs, Roth 401(k)s have **no income limits**. Whether you earn $60,000 or $600,000, you can contribute to a Roth 401(k) if your employer offers one. This is a critical advantage for high earners who are locked out of direct Roth IRA contributions.

**The key assumptions behind choosing Roth:**

- You expect your tax rate in retirement to be the same or higher than today
- You want tax-free income in retirement for planning flexibility
- You want to avoid RMDs on this money (by rolling to a Roth IRA at retirement)
- You believe federal tax rates are more likely to rise than fall over the next 20-30 years
- You have a long time horizon for the tax-free growth to compound

## The Math: When Traditional Wins and When Roth Wins

Let's run a concrete comparison. Assume a 25-year-old contributing $23,500 per year for 35 years with 7% average annual returns, retiring at age 60.

**Scenario 1: Same tax rate (24%) now and in retirement**

| | Traditional 401(k) | Roth 401(k) |
|---|---|---|
| Annual contribution | $23,500 (pre-tax) | $23,500 (after-tax) |
| Tax savings today | $5,640/year | $0 |
| Portfolio value at 60 | $3,648,000 | $3,648,000 |
| Tax on withdrawal | 24% = $875,520 | $0 |
| **After-tax value** | **$2,772,480** | **$3,648,000** |

Wait — it looks like Roth wins by $875,000? Not quite. For a fair comparison, you need to account for the fact that the Traditional contributor saved $5,640 per year in taxes. If they invested that tax savings in a taxable brokerage account at the same 7% return (paying 15% long-term capital gains), that side fund grows to roughly $600,000 after taxes.

**Adjusted comparison:** Traditional ($2,772,480 + ~$600,000 = ~$3,372,480) vs. Roth ($3,648,000). Roth still wins by about $275,000 — and that's at the *same* tax rate. Why? Because the taxable side fund erodes returns through annual dividend taxes and capital gains taxes that the Roth avoids entirely.

**Scenario 2: Tax rate drops from 24% to 15% in retirement**

Traditional pulls ahead. The $3,648,000 portfolio taxed at 15% leaves $3,100,800, plus the ~$600,000 side fund = ~$3,700,000. Traditional wins by about $50,000.

**Scenario 3: Tax rate rises from 24% to 32% in retirement**

Roth wins decisively. The Traditional portfolio taxed at 32% leaves only $2,480,640 + $600,000 = ~$3,080,000 vs. Roth's $3,648,000. Roth wins by nearly $570,000.

**The takeaway:** Roth has a structural advantage because it eliminates tax drag on growth entirely. Traditional only wins meaningfully when your retirement tax rate drops substantially below your current rate.

## Why Most People Underestimate Their Retirement Tax Rate

The conventional wisdom — "you'll be in a lower bracket in retirement" — made more sense 30 years ago. Today, several factors push retirement tax rates higher than people expect:

### 1. Social Security Is Taxable

Up to 85% of your Social Security benefits become taxable income once your "combined income" exceeds $34,000 (single) or $44,000 (married filing jointly). Traditional 401(k) withdrawals count toward this threshold. For a couple withdrawing $80,000 from a Traditional IRA/401(k) plus receiving $40,000 in Social Security, nearly all of that Social Security benefit becomes taxable — an effective marginal rate much higher than the nominal bracket.

### 2. Medicare IRMAA Surcharges

If your Modified Adjusted Gross Income (MAGI) exceeds $106,000 (single) or $212,000 (married) in 2026, you'll pay higher Medicare Part B and Part D premiums — the Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount (IRMAA). These surcharges can add $1,000 to $6,000+ per person per year. Roth withdrawals don't count toward MAGI for IRMAA purposes. Traditional withdrawals do.

### 3. Required Minimum Distributions Force Taxable Income

Starting at age 73 (75 for those born 1960+), you must take RMDs from Traditional accounts whether you need the money or not. A $3 million Traditional 401(k) at age 75 requires roughly a $120,000 annual withdrawal — taxable as ordinary income — even if you have plenty of other income sources. This can push you into higher brackets precisely when you'd rather control your tax exposure.

### 4. Tax Rates May Rise

The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) provisions are scheduled to sunset after 2025, which would push the 22% bracket back to 25%, the 24% bracket back to 28%, and so on. While Congress may extend some provisions, the long-term trajectory of federal tax rates — driven by Social Security and Medicare funding gaps, rising national debt, and demographic pressures — points upward, not downward.

## SECURE 2.0 Changes That Affect This Decision in 2026

The SECURE 2.0 Act introduced several provisions directly relevant to the Roth vs. Traditional choice:

### Mandatory Roth Catch-Up Contributions for High Earners

Starting in 2026, employees who earned more than $145,000 in FICA wages the prior year must make all catch-up contributions (the extra $7,500 for those 50+) on a Roth basis. There's no Traditional option for catch-up contributions if you're a high earner. This means the Roth vs. Traditional decision partially makes itself for workers over 50 earning above this threshold.

### Employer Match Can Go to Roth

Employers can now deposit matching contributions directly into a Roth 401(k) account. Previously, all employer matches went to Traditional regardless of your election. If your employer offers Roth matching, those matched dollars also grow and are withdrawn tax-free — a significant compounding benefit over decades.

### No RMDs on Roth 401(k) Balances

As of 2024, Roth 401(k) accounts are no longer subject to RMDs during the original owner's lifetime, aligning them with Roth IRA rules. This removes a major planning friction that previously required rolling Roth 401(k) balances into a Roth IRA to avoid forced distributions.

## A Framework for Making the Decision

Rather than a single rule, use this decision matrix based on your specific situation:

### Choose Roth 401(k) If:

- You're early in your career and in the 12% or 22% bracket — your income (and tax rate) will almost certainly rise
- You expect total retirement income to exceed your current income (pension, Social Security, real estate income, investment income)
- You want flexibility to control taxable income in retirement (managing IRMAA, ACA subsidies, or Social Security taxation)
- You believe tax rates will increase over the next 20-30 years
- You want to leave tax-free assets to heirs (Roth accounts passed to beneficiaries maintain their tax-free status)
- You're already maxing out contributions and want to effectively shelter more money (a $23,500 Roth contribution shelters more purchasing power than a $23,500 Traditional contribution)

### Choose Traditional 401(k) If:

- You're in the 32% bracket or higher and confident your retirement income will put you in the 22-24% range
- You're within 5-10 years of retirement and need the tax deduction to fund other financial goals
- You live in a high-tax state now but plan to retire in a no-income-tax state (Florida, Texas, Nevada, etc.)
- You need to maximize current cash flow (the tax savings from Traditional contributions are meaningful for monthly budgeting)
- Your employer doesn't offer Roth 401(k) contributions (many smaller employers still don't)

### Split Your Contributions If:

- You're uncertain about future tax rates — hedging by contributing some to Roth and some to Traditional creates tax diversification
- You want both a current-year tax deduction and some tax-free retirement income
- You're in the 22-24% bracket, where the decision is genuinely close

---

## The Split Strategy: Why Tax Diversification Matters

For many workers, the best answer isn't all-or-nothing. Contributing 50/50 or 60/40 between Roth and Traditional gives you optionality in retirement that's worth more than optimizing for a single tax assumption.

With both Roth and Traditional buckets in retirement, you can:

- **Draw from Traditional accounts up to the top of a low bracket** (e.g., fill the 12% bracket with Traditional withdrawals), then cover remaining expenses from Roth — paying very low effective tax rates
- **Manage IRMAA thresholds** by using Roth withdrawals in years when Traditional income would push you over a cliff
- **Smooth Roth conversions** during low-income years (early retirement, sabbatical, job transition) by converting Traditional balances at favorable rates
- **Adapt to tax law changes** — whatever Congress does, you have a bucket that benefits

This is the approach most financial planners recommend for clients in the 22-24% bracket where the Roth vs. Traditional math is genuinely uncertain.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

**Mistake 1: Comparing Roth and Traditional at face value.** A $23,500 Roth contribution is worth more than a $23,500 Traditional contribution because the Roth amount is after-tax. You're effectively sheltering more real dollars in a Roth if you're maxing out the contribution limit.

**Mistake 2: Ignoring state taxes in the analysis.** If you currently live in California (13.3% top rate) and plan to retire in Florida (0%), the Traditional deduction is worth significantly more — you're deducting at 37%+ combined federal and state rates and potentially withdrawing at 22-24% federal only.

**Mistake 3: Assuming your tax rate automatically drops in retirement.** Between Social Security taxation, RMDs, IRMAA, pension income, and potential investment income, many retirees find themselves in the same or higher bracket than during their working years.

**Mistake 4: Not accounting for Roth conversion opportunities.** If you anticipate a gap year, early retirement bridge period, or other low-income window, contributing Traditional now and converting to Roth during those low-income years can be the best of both worlds.

**Mistake 5: Letting the employer match decision drive your contribution choice.** Your employer match goes to Traditional by default (unless your plan allows Roth matching). This means you already have Traditional dollars growing — which may argue for directing your own contributions to Roth for diversification.

## Action Steps for 2026

1. **Check your plan's options.** Log into your 401(k) provider and confirm whether Roth 401(k) is available. If it isn't, ask your HR department — adoption has accelerated, with over 90% of large employers now offering it.

2. **Calculate your marginal rate.** Know your current federal and state marginal tax bracket. Use your most recent tax return — not your W-2 — since deductions affect your marginal rate.

3. **Project your retirement income.** Add up expected Social Security, pension income, rental income, and projected Traditional account RMDs. If the total puts you near or above your current bracket, lean Roth.

4. **Consider the split approach.** If you're in the 22-24% federal bracket and unsure, start with a 50/50 Roth/Traditional split. You can adjust annually based on income changes.

5. **Review catch-up eligibility.** If you're 50+ and earned over $145,000 last year, your catch-up contributions must be Roth starting in 2026. Plan for the cash flow impact of losing that tax deduction on the catch-up portion.

6. **Think about heirs.** If leaving a financial legacy is important, Roth assets are dramatically more efficient to pass on — beneficiaries pay zero income tax on inherited Roth accounts (though they must still draw them down within 10 years under the SECURE Act).

## The Bottom Line

The Roth 401(k) vs. Traditional 401(k) decision isn't about finding a universal answer — it's about understanding your specific tax trajectory and building flexibility into your retirement plan. For most workers under 40 in the 22% or lower bracket, Roth is the clear choice. For high earners in the 35-37% bracket planning to relocate to a low-tax state, Traditional likely wins. For everyone in between, a split strategy provides the tax diversification that lets you optimize withdrawals no matter what Congress does between now and your retirement.

The worst choice is the one you make on autopilot. Run your numbers, make a deliberate decision, and revisit it every year when your income or tax situation changes. Your future self — and your tax bill — will thank you.
