# Securities-Based Lending: How to Borrow Against Your Portfolio Without Selling

You need $400,000 for a down payment on a second home. Or $250,000 to fund a business. Or $150,000 to cover a tax bill while waiting for a liquidity event. The money is there — sitting in your brokerage account, in stocks that have appreciated enormously. But selling would trigger a capital gains tax bill of $80,000 or more, and you'd lose your compounding position in the process.

There's another option that most retail investors never consider: **borrow against your portfolio instead of selling it.**

Securities-based lending (SBL) — sometimes called a pledged asset line, portfolio line of credit, or securities-backed line of credit (SBLOC) — lets you use your investment holdings as collateral for a loan. You get liquidity. You keep your investments. You avoid the tax event. And in many cases, the interest you pay is less than the tax you would have owed.

This strategy sits in every private banker's toolkit, but it's increasingly available to investors with portfolios as small as $100,000. Here's how it works, when it makes sense, and the risks you absolutely must understand before using it.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Securities-based loans** let you borrow 50–95% of your portfolio's value without selling investments or triggering capital gains taxes.
- **Interest rates** on SBLOCs typically run 1–3% above the secured overnight financing rate (SOFR), making them significantly cheaper than unsecured personal loans, credit cards, or even many mortgages.
- **The biggest risk is a margin call** — if your portfolio declines sharply, you may be forced to deposit cash, add collateral, or watch the lender liquidate your holdings at the worst possible time.
- **SBLOCs work best** for short-to-medium-term liquidity needs when you have a well-diversified portfolio, a clear repayment plan, and enough assets outside the pledge to absorb a downturn.
- **They are not free money.** Overleveraging your portfolio amplifies downside risk and can create catastrophic outcomes in a market crash.
- **Tax treatment is favorable** — loan proceeds are not taxable income, and interest may be deductible depending on how you use the funds.

## How Securities-Based Lending Works

The mechanics are straightforward. You pledge your taxable brokerage account (stocks, bonds, ETFs, mutual funds) as collateral. The lender extends a revolving line of credit based on a percentage of the portfolio's value. You draw funds as needed, pay interest on what you borrow, and repay on your own schedule — there's typically no fixed repayment term.

### Advance rates by asset type

The amount you can borrow depends on what you hold. Lenders assign different **advance rates** (also called loan-to-value ratios) based on the volatility and liquidity of each asset class:

| Asset Type | Typical Advance Rate |
|---|---|
| U.S. Treasury bonds | 90–95% |
| Investment-grade corporate bonds | 80–90% |
| S&P 500 / large-cap stocks | 65–75% |
| Diversified equity ETFs | 65–75% |
| Mid-cap stocks | 50–65% |
| Small-cap / volatile stocks | 40–50% |
| Concentrated single stock | 30–50% |
| Municipal bonds | 70–85% |
| International equities | 50–65% |

A well-diversified $1 million portfolio of large-cap stocks and bond ETFs might support a credit line of $650,000 to $750,000. A concentrated position in a single volatile tech stock might only support $300,000 to $500,000.

### Interest rates in 2026

As of mid-2026, SBLOC rates at major brokerages typically range from **5.5% to 7.5%** for most borrowers, depending on loan size, the lending institution, and the borrower's relationship tier. Larger loans command better rates:

| Loan Size | Typical Rate Range |
|---|---|
| $100,000–$250,000 | SOFR + 2.5–3.0% |
| $250,000–$1,000,000 | SOFR + 1.5–2.5% |
| $1,000,000–$5,000,000 | SOFR + 1.0–1.5% |
| $5,000,000+ | SOFR + 0.5–1.0% |

Compare that to a home equity line of credit at 8–9%, a personal loan at 10–14%, or credit card debt at 20%+. On a pure cost-of-capital basis, SBLOCs are among the cheapest forms of borrowing available to individual investors.

## The Tax Arbitrage: Why Borrowing Can Beat Selling

The core value proposition of securities-based lending is **tax deferral**. Here's a concrete example.

**Scenario:** You need $500,000 for a real estate down payment. You hold $1 million in stocks with a cost basis of $300,000 — meaning $700,000 in unrealized long-term capital gains.

**Option A: Sell stock**
- Sell $500,000 of stock, realizing approximately $350,000 in gains
- Federal capital gains tax (20%): $70,000
- Net Investment Income Tax (3.8%): $13,300
- State tax (California, 13.3%): $46,550
- **Total tax: ~$129,850**
- You receive approximately $370,150 after tax — you need to sell even more to reach $500,000

**Option B: Borrow against portfolio**
- Borrow $500,000 via SBLOC at 6.5% interest
- Annual interest cost: $32,500
- You keep the full $1 million invested
- No capital gains tax triggered
- **Year one cost: $32,500** (versus $129,850+ in taxes)

Even after three years of interest payments ($97,500 total), you've spent less than you would have in taxes — and your portfolio has continued to compound. If your stocks return 8–10% annually, the $500,000 you didn't sell has generated an additional $125,000–$158,000 in unrealized growth over those three years.

The math is especially compelling when:
- Your unrealized gains are large relative to your cost basis
- You're in a high-tax state (California, New York, New Jersey)
- You expect to sell later at a lower tax rate (e.g., after retirement, after moving to a no-income-tax state)
- You plan to hold until death, when heirs receive a stepped-up basis and the gains disappear entirely

## Where to Get a Securities-Based Loan

Most major brokerages and private banks offer SBLOCs, though availability and terms vary:

### Full-service brokerages and private banks
- **Morgan Stanley** — Liquidity Access Line. Available to clients with $100,000+ in eligible securities.
- **Goldman Sachs / Marcus** — GS Select for private wealth clients. Competitive rates for $1M+ portfolios.
- **J.P. Morgan Private Bank** — Pledged asset lines for high-net-worth clients. Best rates in the industry for ultra-high-net-worth relationships.
- **UBS, Merrill Lynch, Wells Fargo Advisors** — All offer proprietary SBLOC products through their wealth management divisions.

### Self-directed brokerages
- **Schwab** — Pledged Asset Line (PAL). Available to clients with $100,000+ in eligible assets. Rates are competitive and the application process is straightforward.
- **Fidelity** — Securities-based line of credit through Fidelity Brokerage Services. Generally requires $100,000+.
- **Interactive Brokers** — Margin loans (not technically an SBLOC, but functionally similar) with some of the lowest rates in the industry — often under SOFR + 1.5% for large balances.

### Important distinction: SBLOC vs. margin loan

These terms are often used interchangeably, but they're different products:

| Feature | SBLOC / Pledged Asset Line | Margin Loan |
|---|---|---|
| Regulated by | Banking regulations (OCC) | SEC / FINRA (Regulation T) |
| Purpose restrictions | Any purpose except buying securities | Must be used to buy securities |
| Advance rate | Varies by lender, often higher | Max 50% for initial purchase (Reg T) |
| Margin call rules | Lender-specific maintenance requirements | FINRA minimum 25% maintenance margin |
| Interest deductibility | Depends on use of proceeds | Investment interest deduction (limited) |
| Account type | Separate lending facility | Same brokerage account |

For most non-investment liquidity needs (home purchase, business funding, bridge loan), an SBLOC is the appropriate product. If you're borrowing to buy more securities, you're using margin.

## The Risks You Must Understand

Securities-based lending is not a free lunch. The risks are real, and they've burned investors who underestimated them — particularly during the 2020 COVID crash, the 2022 bear market, and any sudden market correction.

### Risk 1: Margin calls and forced liquidation

This is the critical risk. If your portfolio declines in value, the lender's loan-to-value ratio increases. When it crosses the lender's maintenance threshold (which varies by institution but is often around 80–90% of the credit line), you'll face a **margin call** — a demand to either:

1. Deposit additional cash or securities
2. Pay down the loan balance
3. Allow the lender to liquidate your holdings

The lender is not required to give you advance warning, wait for a market recovery, or liquidate your least-tax-efficient positions. They can sell whatever they want, whenever they want, to bring the loan back into compliance. If the market drops 30% in a week (as it did in March 2020), you could face a forced sale at the bottom — realizing the very capital gains you were trying to avoid, at the worst possible prices.

**Rule of thumb:** Never borrow more than 40–50% of your advance rate. If you're approved for a $700,000 line, keep your actual borrowing below $350,000. This gives your portfolio room to decline 30–40% before you approach a margin call.

### Risk 2: Rising interest rates

SBLOCs are variable-rate products. If interest rates rise significantly, your borrowing costs increase with no ceiling. A loan that costs 6% today could cost 8% or 9% if the Fed raises rates — and unlike a mortgage, there's no option to lock in a fixed rate.

### Risk 3: Portfolio underperformance

The tax arbitrage only works if your portfolio earns more than the interest rate on the loan. If you borrow at 6.5% and your portfolio returns 3% in a given year, you've lost money on the spread. Over a sustained period of poor returns, the compounding cost of the loan can exceed what you would have paid in taxes.

### Risk 4: Behavioral risk

Easy access to cheap leverage can encourage overspending. What starts as a bridge loan for a specific purpose can quietly become a permanent fixture — a line you never fully pay down because the portfolio "covers it." This is how people end up over-leveraged without realizing it.

### Risk 5: Estate and bankruptcy considerations

In some jurisdictions, a pledged asset line may complicate estate planning because the lender's security interest takes priority over heirs' claims. In bankruptcy, the lender can seize and liquidate the pledged securities ahead of other creditors. Consult an estate attorney before pledging assets that form a core part of your estate plan.

## When Securities-Based Lending Makes Sense

SBLOCs are a powerful tool in specific situations. They are not appropriate for everyone.

### Good use cases

- **Bridge financing:** You need short-term liquidity while waiting for a home sale, business exit, or restricted stock vesting. The loan is temporary, and repayment is clearly funded.
- **Tax arbitrage on highly appreciated stock:** Your unrealized gains are so large that the tax cost of selling exceeds several years of interest payments — especially if you plan to eventually reset your basis through gifting, charitable giving, or the stepped-up basis at death.
- **Real estate down payment:** Using an SBLOC for a down payment while keeping your portfolio invested, then refinancing or repaying once the property purchase settles.
- **Business investment:** Funding a business opportunity where the expected return exceeds the borrowing cost and the time horizon is defined.
- **Tax bill smoothing:** Covering a large tax liability (from Roth conversions, RSU vesting, or business income) without forced selling in a down market.

### Poor use cases

- **Funding ongoing lifestyle expenses.** If you're borrowing to cover spending you can't otherwise afford, the loan will grow indefinitely. This ends badly.
- **Concentrated, volatile portfolios.** Pledging a single stock or a portfolio of high-beta tech names magnifies the margin call risk to unacceptable levels.
- **No repayment plan.** If you can't articulate exactly how and when you'll repay the loan, you shouldn't take it.
- **Near-retirement with limited other assets.** If the pledged portfolio is also your retirement income source, a margin call could compromise your retirement security.

## Tax Treatment of Securities-Based Loans

Loan proceeds from an SBLOC are **not taxable income**. You're borrowing, not earning — so there's no tax event when you draw funds. This is the fundamental advantage over selling.

Interest deductibility depends on what you do with the proceeds:

| Use of Loan Proceeds | Interest Deductibility |
|---|---|
| Buying investment property | Deductible as investment interest (limited to net investment income) |
| Business purposes | Deductible as business expense |
| Personal use (home purchase, consumer spending) | Generally not deductible |
| Buying tax-exempt securities (munis) | Not deductible |

If you use the funds for investment purposes, the interest is deductible against net investment income under IRC Section 163(d). You'll need to trace the use of funds carefully — the IRS follows the "tracing rules" to determine deductibility based on how the borrowed money was actually used, not on how the loan was structured.

## A Practical Framework for Using SBLOCs Responsibly

If you've determined that securities-based lending is appropriate for your situation, here are the guardrails that keep the strategy safe:

1. **Keep your loan-to-value ratio below 25% of your total portfolio.** This provides a massive cushion against market declines. At 25% LTV, the market would need to fall roughly 50% before you'd approach a margin call — well beyond a typical correction.

2. **Maintain liquid reserves outside the pledged account.** Keep enough cash or bonds in a separate, unpledged account to cover at least 12 months of interest payments and a potential margin call top-up.

3. **Set a hard repayment timeline.** Treat the SBLOC like a term loan with a target payoff date, even though it technically has no maturity. Review the balance quarterly and pay it down aggressively.

4. **Diversify the collateral.** A portfolio of 50+ positions across sectors and asset classes is far more resilient as collateral than a handful of concentrated bets.

5. **Stress-test the worst case.** Before borrowing, calculate what happens to your loan and your margin if the portfolio drops 30%, 40%, or 50%. If any of those scenarios creates a financial emergency, reduce the loan amount until the stress test is survivable.

6. **Don't borrow to invest.** Using an SBLOC to buy more stocks creates leveraged exposure that amplifies both gains and losses. This is speculation, not liquidity management.

## The Bottom Line

Securities-based lending is one of the most powerful — and most misunderstood — tools in personal finance. Used correctly, it lets you access liquidity without disrupting your investment strategy or triggering unnecessary taxes. Used recklessly, it amplifies risk in exactly the way that destroys wealth during market downturns.

The strategy works best for investors with diversified portfolios, significant unrealized gains, a specific liquidity need, and the discipline to borrow conservatively and repay systematically. It's not a substitute for proper financial planning — it's an addition to it.

Before opening an SBLOC, run the numbers with your financial advisor and tax professional. Compare the total cost of borrowing (interest payments over the expected loan term) against the total cost of selling (capital gains tax plus lost compounding). In many cases, particularly for high-net-worth investors in high-tax states, the math strongly favors borrowing. But the math only works if you respect the risks.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I use retirement accounts (IRA, 401(k)) as collateral for an SBLOC?

No. Federal law prohibits using retirement accounts as collateral for a loan. If you pledge IRA assets, the IRS treats it as a distribution — triggering income taxes and potentially early withdrawal penalties. SBLOCs are exclusively for taxable brokerage accounts.

### What happens to my SBLOC if I die?

The loan becomes part of your estate's liabilities. Your heirs inherit the pledged securities at a stepped-up cost basis (eliminating the unrealized gains), but they also inherit the loan obligation. The estate will need to repay the loan, either from other assets or by selling the securities — but because of the stepped-up basis, the sale would generate little or no capital gains tax. This is one reason SBLOCs pair well with estate planning strategies.

### Can I still trade in my pledged account?

Generally yes, with restrictions. Most lenders allow you to buy and sell within the pledged account as long as the account value remains above the maintenance threshold. However, withdrawing cash or transferring securities out of the account usually requires lender approval.

### Is an SBLOC the same as buying on margin?

No. Margin loans are used to purchase additional securities within your brokerage account and are regulated by the SEC and FINRA. SBLOCs are non-purpose loans (they cannot be used to buy securities) regulated by banking authorities. The advance rates, maintenance requirements, and regulatory frameworks are different.

### How quickly can I access funds from an SBLOC?

Once the line is established (which typically takes 5–10 business days for initial setup), draws are usually available within one to three business days. Some lenders offer same-day or next-day funding for established lines.
