## Stock Valuation Metrics Explained: How to Use P/E Ratio, PEG Ratio, Price-to-Book, and DCF to Find Undervalued Stocks in 2026

Every investor eventually faces the same question: is this stock actually worth what the market says it's worth?

The price of a stock tells you what people are willing to pay today. It tells you nothing about whether that price makes sense. A $500 stock can be cheap. A $12 stock can be wildly expensive. The difference between the two comes down to what the underlying business earns, owns, and is expected to generate in the future — and how those fundamentals compare to the price tag the market has attached.

This is where valuation metrics come in. They're not crystal balls. They won't tell you exactly when to buy or sell. But they give you a structured way to compare what you're paying against what you're getting — and that discipline, applied consistently, is what separates investors who build wealth from those who chase headlines.

Here's how to use the most important valuation tools available to individual investors in 2026, when to rely on each one, and where they can mislead you.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **The P/E ratio is the starting point, not the destination** — it tells you how much you're paying per dollar of earnings, but it ignores growth, debt, and capital intensity.
- **The PEG ratio adjusts P/E for growth** and is especially useful for comparing companies growing at different rates within the same sector.
- **Price-to-book value shines for asset-heavy businesses** like banks, insurers, and REITs, but is nearly useless for asset-light tech companies.
- **EV/EBITDA is the professional's workhorse** — it accounts for debt and provides a cleaner comparison across companies with different capital structures.
- **Discounted cash flow (DCF) analysis is the most rigorous approach** but requires assumptions about the future that can make or break the conclusion.
- **No single metric works in isolation** — the best investors use multiple valuation lenses and compare against sector-specific benchmarks, not absolute thresholds.

## The P/E Ratio: Your First Filter, Not Your Last

The price-to-earnings ratio is the most widely quoted valuation metric in finance. It divides the current share price by the company's earnings per share (EPS) over the trailing twelve months (TTM) or the expected earnings for the next twelve months (forward P/E).

**Formula:** P/E = Share Price ÷ Earnings Per Share

If a company trades at $150 per share and earned $10 per share last year, its trailing P/E is 15x. You're paying $15 for every $1 of annual profit the business generates.

### What the P/E Ratio Tells You

A lower P/E generally means the market expects less future growth — or perceives more risk. A higher P/E means the market is pricing in faster growth, competitive advantages, or both. The S&P 500's long-run average P/E sits around 16–17x. As of late 2026, the index trades closer to 22x forward earnings, reflecting AI-driven productivity expectations and a still-resilient economy.

### Where the P/E Ratio Breaks Down

The P/E ratio has three significant blind spots:

1. **It ignores growth.** A company growing earnings at 30% annually deserves a higher P/E than one growing at 5%. Comparing their P/E ratios head-to-head without accounting for growth is like comparing a 25-year-old's earning potential to a retiree's.

2. **Earnings can be manipulated.** Companies can boost EPS through share buybacks, one-time gains, favorable depreciation schedules, or aggressive revenue recognition. Always look at the quality of earnings, not just the number.

3. **It doesn't account for debt.** A company with $10 billion in debt and one with $10 billion in cash can have identical P/E ratios but wildly different risk profiles.

**Best used for:** Quick comparisons between companies in the same industry with similar growth rates and capital structures. Consumer staples, utilities, and mature industrials are sectors where P/E comparisons are most reliable.

## The PEG Ratio: Adjusting for Growth

Peter Lynch popularized the PEG ratio in his book *One Up on Wall Street*, and it remains one of the most practical tools for growth-oriented investors.

**Formula:** PEG = P/E Ratio ÷ Annual EPS Growth Rate

If a stock has a P/E of 30 and is growing earnings at 30% per year, its PEG is 1.0. If another stock has a P/E of 15 but is only growing at 5%, its PEG is 3.0. Despite having a lower P/E, the slower-growing company is actually more expensive relative to its growth.

### How to Interpret PEG

| PEG Range | Interpretation |
|-----------|---------------|
| Below 0.75 | Potentially undervalued relative to growth |
| 0.75 – 1.0 | Fairly valued relative to growth |
| 1.0 – 1.5 | Moderately priced — growth may justify it |
| Above 1.5 | Potentially overvalued unless growth accelerates |

### The PEG Ratio's Limitations

The PEG ratio depends entirely on the growth estimate you plug in. Use trailing growth, and you're looking in the rearview mirror. Use analyst consensus forward estimates, and you're relying on forecasts that are frequently wrong — analysts have historically overestimated earnings growth by an average of 2–3 percentage points annually.

The PEG ratio also struggles with low-growth or cyclical companies. A utility growing earnings at 2% with a P/E of 14 has a PEG of 7.0 — but that doesn't mean it's seven times overvalued. It means the PEG ratio simply isn't the right tool for that type of business.

**Best used for:** Comparing growth stocks within the same sector, especially in technology, healthcare, and consumer discretionary where earnings growth is the primary value driver.

## Price-to-Book (P/B) Ratio: What Are the Assets Really Worth?

The price-to-book ratio compares a company's market capitalization to its book value — the net assets on the balance sheet (total assets minus total liabilities).

**Formula:** P/B = Share Price ÷ Book Value Per Share

A P/B below 1.0 means the market values the company at less than the accounting value of its assets. In theory, you could buy the entire company, liquidate its assets, and make a profit. In practice, it usually signals that the market expects future losses, or that the book value overstates the true worth of those assets.

### When Price-to-Book Works

P/B is most useful for industries where the balance sheet closely approximates the company's actual economic value:

- **Banks and financial institutions** — their assets are primarily loans, securities, and cash, all of which have clear market values.
- **Insurance companies** — their investment portfolios and reserves make book value a meaningful anchor.
- **REITs and real estate companies** — tangible property holdings give book value real-world significance.

### When It Doesn't

For asset-light businesses — software companies, consulting firms, brand-driven consumer companies — book value is almost meaningless. Microsoft's most valuable assets are its intellectual property, brand, customer relationships, and recurring revenue streams. None of these appear on the balance sheet at anything close to their true value. Microsoft's P/B ratio routinely exceeds 10x, but that doesn't make it overvalued.

**Best used for:** Valuing financial institutions, REITs, and other asset-heavy businesses. The long-run average P/B for the S&P 500 Financials sector is approximately 1.3–1.5x.

## EV/EBITDA: The Professional's Preferred Metric

Enterprise value to EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization) is the metric most commonly used by institutional investors, private equity firms, and M&A analysts. It solves two major problems with the P/E ratio.

**Formula:** EV/EBITDA = Enterprise Value ÷ EBITDA

Enterprise value equals market capitalization plus total debt minus cash and equivalents. By using EV instead of market cap, you're accounting for the company's full capital structure — what it would actually cost to acquire the entire business, debts and all.

EBITDA strips out the effects of financing decisions (interest), tax jurisdictions (taxes), and accounting choices (depreciation and amortization) to give you a cleaner picture of operational profitability.

### EV/EBITDA Benchmarks by Sector

| Sector | Typical EV/EBITDA Range |
|--------|------------------------|
| Technology | 15–30x |
| Healthcare | 12–20x |
| Consumer Staples | 10–15x |
| Industrials | 8–14x |
| Energy | 4–8x |
| Utilities | 8–12x |
| Financials | Not typically used (use P/B or P/E) |

### Why Professionals Prefer It

If Company A has $2 billion in debt and Company B has $2 billion in cash, but both trade at the same market cap and have the same EBITDA, their P/E ratios might look similar. Their EV/EBITDA ratios will reveal that Company A is substantially more expensive because its enterprise value is $4 billion higher.

**Best used for:** Comparing companies with different levels of debt, across industries, and especially for evaluating acquisition targets. It's the single most versatile valuation metric available.

## Discounted Cash Flow (DCF): The Gold Standard

A DCF analysis estimates the intrinsic value of a company by projecting its future free cash flows and discounting them back to their present value. It's the most theoretically rigorous valuation method — and the most dangerous if you don't understand its sensitivity to assumptions.

### The DCF Process (Simplified)

1. **Estimate free cash flow (FCF)** for the next 5–10 years based on revenue growth, margins, capital expenditures, and working capital needs.
2. **Calculate a terminal value** — the value of all cash flows beyond your projection period, typically using a perpetual growth rate of 2–3%.
3. **Choose a discount rate** — usually the company's weighted average cost of capital (WACC), which blends the cost of equity and the cost of debt. For most publicly traded companies, WACC ranges from 8–12%.
4. **Sum the present values** of all projected cash flows and the terminal value. Divide by shares outstanding to get intrinsic value per share.

### A Simplified Example

Suppose a company generates $500 million in free cash flow this year, and you expect FCF to grow at 10% annually for 5 years, then 3% perpetually. Using a 10% discount rate:

| Year | Projected FCF | Present Value |
|------|--------------|---------------|
| 1 | $550M | $500M |
| 2 | $605M | $500M |
| 3 | $666M | $500M |
| 4 | $732M | $500M |
| 5 | $805M | $500M |
| Terminal Value | $11,844M | $7,354M |
| **Total Intrinsic Value** | | **$9,854M** |

If the company's market cap is $8 billion, the DCF suggests it's trading at roughly a 19% discount to intrinsic value — potentially undervalued.

### The Critical Caveat

Small changes in assumptions produce enormous swings in output. Changing the discount rate from 10% to 9% can increase your intrinsic value estimate by 20–30%. Changing the terminal growth rate from 3% to 4% can add another 15–20%. This is why professional analysts present DCF results as a range, not a single number, and why you should always run sensitivity analyses on your key assumptions.

**Best used for:** Deep analysis of individual stock picks, especially for investors willing to spend time building financial models. Most useful for mature companies with predictable cash flows (consumer staples, industrials, healthcare).

## How to Combine These Metrics: A Practical Framework

No single metric tells the whole story. Here's a practical approach to stock valuation that uses multiple lenses:

### Step 1: Start with the Sector

Different sectors deserve different primary metrics. Use P/E and PEG for growth companies, P/B for financials, EV/EBITDA for industrials, and DCF for your highest-conviction individual picks.

### Step 2: Compare Against Peers

Never evaluate a stock's valuation in absolute terms. A P/E of 25 is cheap for a cloud software company growing at 30% and expensive for a utility growing at 3%. Always benchmark against the sector median and the company's own 5-year historical range.

### Step 3: Look for Valuation Convergence

When multiple metrics point in the same direction — low P/E relative to peers, PEG below 1.0, EV/EBITDA below the sector average, and DCF showing a 15%+ margin of safety — you have convergent evidence of undervaluation. When the metrics disagree, dig deeper to understand why.

### Step 4: Check the Quality of the Underlying Numbers

Before trusting any ratio, verify the inputs. Are earnings being inflated by one-time items? Is free cash flow sustainable, or was it boosted by delaying capital expenditures? Is the growth rate realistic given the company's competitive position and market size? Is the balance sheet carrying hidden liabilities?

## Common Valuation Mistakes to Avoid

**Anchoring to a single metric.** A stock with a low P/E can still be a terrible investment if earnings are declining, the balance sheet is deteriorating, or management is destroying value through poor capital allocation.

**Using trailing metrics for cyclical companies.** At the peak of an economic cycle, cyclical companies (autos, homebuilders, miners) have high earnings and low P/E ratios — right before earnings collapse. Use normalized or mid-cycle earnings instead.

**Ignoring the difference between "cheap" and "value trap."** Stocks trade at low valuations for a reason. Before buying a statistically cheap stock, make sure you understand what the market is worried about — and have a specific reason to believe those worries are overdone.

**Applying tech multiples to non-tech businesses.** A SaaS company with 95% gross margins and 120% net revenue retention deserves a higher EV/EBITDA than a manufacturing company with 30% gross margins and no pricing power. Valuation multiples only make sense within their context.

---

## The Bottom Line

Valuation metrics are tools, not answers. They help you ask better questions: Is this company priced for perfection, or does the market underestimate its potential? Am I paying a fair price for growth, or am I paying a premium that requires everything to go right?

The investors who consistently outperform aren't the ones who memorize formulas — they're the ones who develop the judgment to know which metric matters most in each situation, and the discipline to walk away when the numbers don't support the story.

Start with P/E and PEG for quick filtering. Use EV/EBITDA for deeper cross-company comparisons. Build a DCF model for your highest-conviction positions. And always remember that the best valuation analysis in the world is worthless without patience and the willingness to buy when others are fearful — and sell when the crowd is euphoric.
