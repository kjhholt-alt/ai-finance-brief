## Year-End Tax Planning Checklist: 15 Smart Moves Before December 31

October hits and most people aren't thinking about taxes yet. That's a mistake. The window between now and December 31 is when the highest-impact tax moves can be made — and most of them can't be done retroactively once January arrives.

Whether you're a W-2 employee, self-employed, a retiree managing distributions, or an investor sitting on gains and losses, there are concrete steps you can take in the next 12 weeks that could save you thousands. Some of these are well-known. Others are routinely overlooked, even by people with accountants.

This isn't theoretical. Every item below is something you can act on this quarter.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **The October-to-December window** is your last chance to optimize deductions, manage capital gains, and adjust retirement contributions for the 2026 tax year.
- **Roth conversions, tax-loss harvesting, and charitable giving strategies** all have hard December 31 deadlines — miss them and you wait another year.
- **SECURE 2.0 provisions** continue rolling out in 2026, creating new opportunities for catch-up contributions and employer match Roth elections.
- **Estimated tax payments** for Q4 are due January 15, 2027 — but the planning happens now.
- **Coordination matters more than any single tactic.** A Roth conversion that pushes you into a higher bracket can undo the savings from your charitable deduction. Model the full picture.

## 1. Max Out Your 401(k) and 403(b) Contributions

The 2026 employee deferral limit for 401(k) and 403(b) plans is $24,000 ($23,500 in 2025, adjusted for inflation). If you're 50 or older, the catch-up contribution adds $7,500, bringing your ceiling to $31,500.

Under SECURE 2.0, workers aged 60 to 63 get an enhanced "super catch-up" of $11,250, pushing the total to $35,250 for that narrow age band.

**Action item:** Log into your payroll system and check your year-to-date contributions. If you're behind, increase your deferral percentage for the remaining pay periods. Most plans allow changes within one to two pay cycles.

---

## 2. Evaluate a Roth Conversion

If your income is lower than usual this year — maybe you took a sabbatical, changed jobs, or had a business loss — this could be an ideal year to convert some or all of a traditional IRA to a Roth.

You'll pay ordinary income tax on the converted amount now, but future growth and withdrawals are tax-free. The math works best when you can convert at a lower marginal rate than you expect to face in retirement.

**Key constraint:** You have until December 31 to execute the conversion for the 2026 tax year. There's no extension. And unlike contributions, Roth conversions can no longer be recharacterized (undone) — so model carefully before pulling the trigger.

**Action item:** Run a tax projection with your CPA or tax software. Compare your marginal rate this year to your expected rate in retirement. If this year's rate is lower, conversion could be a significant long-term win.

---

## 3. Harvest Your Tax Losses

Tax-loss harvesting is the single most actionable move for taxable brokerage accounts. If you hold positions with unrealized losses, selling them before year-end lets you offset capital gains dollar-for-dollar. If your losses exceed your gains, you can deduct up to $3,000 against ordinary income, with the excess carried forward indefinitely.

**Watch the wash sale rule:** If you buy a "substantially identical" security within 30 days before or after the sale, the loss is disallowed. You can avoid this by swapping into a similar but not identical fund — for example, replacing a total U.S. market ETF with a large-cap blend ETF from a different provider.

**Action item:** Review your taxable accounts for positions with unrealized losses. Prioritize harvesting short-term losses first (they offset short-term gains taxed at ordinary income rates). Pair this with move #4 below.

---

## 4. Offset Gains with Strategic Selling

If you've taken profits this year — or your mutual funds are about to distribute capital gains — harvested losses from move #3 can offset them. But you can also be strategic about *which* gains you realize.

Long-term capital gains (held over one year) are taxed at 0%, 15%, or 20% depending on your income. If your taxable income puts you in the 0% bracket — which for married filers in 2026 extends to roughly $96,700 — you can sell appreciated long-term positions and pay zero federal tax on the gain.

This is called **tax-gain harvesting**, and it's one of the most overlooked year-end strategies. It resets your cost basis higher, reducing future tax liability.

**Action item:** Check whether your taxable income, after deductions, falls within the 0% long-term capital gains bracket. If so, consider deliberately realizing long-term gains before year-end.

---

## 5. Make Your Charitable Contributions Count

If you itemize deductions, charitable giving is a powerful lever — but the strategy matters as much as the amount.

**Donate appreciated stock directly.** If you give shares held for more than a year directly to a qualified charity, you deduct the full fair market value and avoid paying capital gains tax on the appreciation. This is strictly better than selling the stock, paying the tax, and donating the cash.

**Consider bunching.** If your total itemized deductions hover near the standard deduction ($15,700 for single filers, $31,400 for married filing jointly in 2026), consider "bunching" two or more years of charitable giving into a single year using a **donor-advised fund (DAF)**. You take the full deduction this year, then distribute the grants to charities over time.

**Action item:** If you plan to donate, do it with appreciated securities through a DAF or directly to the charity. Coordinate with moves #3 and #4 to optimize your overall capital gains picture.

---

## 6. Take Your Required Minimum Distributions

If you're 73 or older (the SECURE 2.0 threshold), you must take your RMD from traditional IRAs, 401(k)s, and other tax-deferred accounts by December 31. Miss the deadline and you face a 25% excise tax on the amount not withdrawn (reduced from the old 50% penalty, but still painful).

**First-year exception:** If you turned 73 in 2026, you can delay your first RMD until April 1, 2027 — but you'll then have to take two distributions in 2027, which can create a tax spike.

**Action item:** Calculate your RMD using the IRS Uniform Lifetime Table. If you haven't taken it yet, schedule the distribution now to avoid the December rush. Consider whether a Qualified Charitable Distribution (QCD) makes sense (see move #7).

---

## 7. Use Qualified Charitable Distributions If You're 70½+

If you're 70½ or older, you can transfer up to $105,000 directly from your IRA to a qualified charity. This counts toward your RMD but isn't included in your adjusted gross income.

Why does this matter? Lower AGI can reduce your Medicare IRMAA surcharges, lower the taxation of your Social Security benefits, and keep you below thresholds for the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax.

**Action item:** If you're charitably inclined and over 70½, a QCD is almost always more tax-efficient than taking the RMD as income and donating separately. Instruct your IRA custodian to make the transfer directly to the charity.

---

## 8. Fund Your HSA to the Max

If you're enrolled in a high-deductible health plan (HDHP), your Health Savings Account is the most tax-advantaged account available — triple tax-free (deductible going in, tax-free growth, tax-free withdrawals for medical expenses).

The 2026 contribution limits are $4,300 for self-only coverage and $8,550 for family coverage, with an additional $1,000 catch-up if you're 55 or older.

Unlike 401(k)s, you have until April 15, 2027, to make HSA contributions for 2026. But doing it now lets you capture more tax-free growth — and ensures you don't forget.

**Action item:** Check your year-to-date HSA contributions and top up to the maximum. If you're not currently using your HSA for current medical expenses, consider paying out of pocket and letting the HSA grow for retirement.

---

## 9. Review Your Withholding and Estimated Payments

Underpayment penalties are assessed on a quarterly basis. If your income spiked mid-year — from a bonus, stock vesting, a real estate sale, or a side business — your withholding may not cover it.

The fourth quarter estimated tax payment is due January 15, 2027. But if you're a W-2 employee, you can increase your withholding for the remaining pay periods to cover the gap. Withholding is treated as paid evenly throughout the year (unlike estimated payments), which can help you avoid underpayment penalties even if you're catching up late.

**Action item:** Run a quick tax projection. If your total withholding plus estimated payments will fall short of 100% of last year's tax liability (or 110% if your AGI exceeded $150,000), adjust now.

---

## 10. Consider Accelerating or Deferring Income

Depending on your situation, shifting income between 2026 and 2027 can meaningfully change your tax outcome.

**Accelerate income if** you expect to be in a higher tax bracket next year — for example, if you're starting a higher-paying job in January, expecting a large bonus, or anticipating legislative changes that raise rates.

**Defer income if** you expect to be in a lower bracket next year — perhaps you're retiring, taking a leave, or your business income is cyclical.

Self-employed individuals have the most flexibility here: you can time invoicing, accelerate or delay billing, and adjust the timing of major expenses.

**Action item:** Compare your projected 2026 and 2027 marginal tax rates. If there's a meaningful difference, work with your accountant to shift income or deductions accordingly.

---

## 11. Maximize Your 529 Plan Contributions

If you have children or grandchildren, 529 plan contributions offer state tax deductions in over 30 states. There's no federal deduction, but the tax-free growth and withdrawals for qualified education expenses make these powerful vehicles.

Under SECURE 2.0, unused 529 funds can now be rolled into a beneficiary's Roth IRA (up to $35,000 lifetime, subject to annual Roth contribution limits and a 15-year account age requirement). This eliminates the old fear of "overfunding" a 529.

**Action item:** Check whether your state offers a tax deduction for 529 contributions and whether there's a December 31 deadline. Fund before year-end to capture the deduction.

---

## 12. Use Your FSA Balance Before It Expires

If you have a Flexible Spending Account (FSA), most plans operate on a "use it or lose it" basis. Some employers offer a grace period (up to 2.5 months into 2027) or allow a $640 rollover, but the rest is forfeited.

**Action item:** Check your FSA balance and your plan's carryover or grace period rules. Schedule any deferred medical expenses — dental work, new glasses, physical therapy — before the deadline.

---

## 13. Evaluate Gifting Strategies

The annual gift tax exclusion for 2026 is $19,000 per recipient ($38,000 for married couples giving jointly). Gifts within this limit don't require a gift tax return and don't reduce your lifetime estate tax exemption.

If you're making larger transfers, the lifetime exemption remains elevated at approximately $13.99 million per person through 2025 sunset provisions — but this is under active legislative scrutiny. If you're sitting on a large estate and haven't used your exemption, the urgency to act before a potential reduction is real.

**Action item:** If you plan to make gifts, do so before December 31 to use the 2026 annual exclusion. For larger estate planning moves, consult an estate attorney about whether to accelerate transfers before potential exemption reductions.

---

## 14. Review Your Asset Location

Tax-efficient asset location — placing the right investments in the right accounts — is an ongoing optimization, but year-end is the natural checkpoint.

As a general framework:
- **Tax-deferred accounts (traditional IRA, 401(k)):** Hold bonds, REITs, and other high-income-generating assets that would be taxed at ordinary income rates.
- **Tax-free accounts (Roth IRA, Roth 401(k)):** Hold your highest-growth assets — these will never be taxed again.
- **Taxable accounts:** Hold tax-efficient index funds, municipal bonds, and assets you want to harvest losses from.

**Action item:** As you rebalance your portfolio for year-end (move #15), ensure you're not holding high-turnover actively managed funds in taxable accounts or low-growth bonds in your Roth.

---

## 15. Rebalance Your Portfolio

Market movements throughout 2026 have likely shifted your allocation away from your target. Year-end rebalancing brings you back to your intended risk level and can be coordinated with tax-loss harvesting (move #3) and tax-gain harvesting (move #4) to minimize the tax cost.

**Action item:** Compare your current allocation to your target. Execute trades in tax-advantaged accounts first (no tax consequences), then use taxable account trades to harvest losses or gains as appropriate.

---

## The Coordination Problem: Why Sequencing Matters

The biggest mistake in year-end tax planning isn't missing a single move — it's failing to coordinate them. Consider:

- A Roth conversion increases your AGI, which could push your Medicare premiums higher (IRMAA), trigger the Net Investment Income Tax, or phase out other deductions.
- Tax-loss harvesting reduces your taxable income, which could create room for a larger Roth conversion at a lower rate.
- A large charitable contribution via DAF could offset the income from a Roth conversion, making both moves more attractive together than either alone.

**The optimal strategy is a system, not a checklist of independent moves.** Run a comprehensive tax projection that models all 15 moves together before executing any of them.

## Bottom Line

Year-end tax planning isn't glamorous, but the ROI on a few hours of analysis between now and December can be substantial — often more valuable than months of stock-picking or fund selection. The strategies above aren't loopholes; they're the legitimate, structural opportunities built into the tax code that Congress intended taxpayers to use.

The key is acting before December 31. Most of these moves have hard deadlines, and none of them can be done retroactively. Start with a tax projection, identify your highest-impact opportunities, and execute them in the right sequence.

Your future self — the one writing a smaller check to the IRS next April — will thank you.
